Chapter 2 moves through a church roster by category — older men, older women, young women, young men, even slaves (2:1-10) — and it would read as merely conventional if Paul didn't stop, at the end, to explain *why* any of it matters. The instructions aren't manners for their own sake. They're evidence.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible titus 2`

## Teaching that trains, not just informs

Verse 4 has older women "training the young wives" — the word carries the sense of coaching, not lecturing. Titus 2 assumes doctrine is meant to shape ordinary behavior at every stage of life, and that older members of a church are supposed to actively hand this down, not just model it from a distance.

## So that no one has a reason

Twice in the chapter, Paul gives the same rationale for good behavior: so that "God's word may not be blasphemed" (2:5), and so opponents will have "no evil thing to say" (2:8). The concern isn't reputation for its own sake — it's that a watching world's only access to whether the gospel is real is the visible lives of people who claim it.

## Grace as a teacher

The chapter's turn comes in verse 11:

> *"For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all men, instructing us to the intent that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we would live soberly, righteously, and godly in this present age."* — Titus 2:11-12

Grace, here, is not opposed to discipline — it produces it. Being forgiven doesn't loosen the expectation of a changed life; in Paul's grammar, grace is the one doing the instructing.

## Questions for study

1. Where in your life is someone older meant to be "training" you, or you training someone younger (2:3-4)?
2. Does linking your behavior to whether "God's word is blasphemed" (2:5) feel like too much weight, or the right amount?
3. How does 2:11-12 change the relationship between grace and effort in your own thinking?
4. What does it mean, practically, to be "zealous for good works" (2:14) rather than merely avoiding bad ones?
