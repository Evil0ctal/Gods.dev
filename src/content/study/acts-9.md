Saul is on his way to arrest Christians when the light knocks him down, and the question from heaven is oddly personal: *"Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?"* (9:4). Not "my church," not "my people" — me. Jesus identifies so completely with the believers Saul is hunting that an attack on them registers as an attack on him directly. That single pronoun does more theological work than a paragraph could.

## An errand Ananias doesn't want

Ananias, told to go heal the very man terrorizing the church, objects with the obvious argument: he's heard "how much evil he did to your saints" (9:13). God doesn't argue him out of the fear — he simply repeats the assignment, adding that Saul will now suffer for the name he once tried to erase (9:15-16). Obedience here means going anyway, not going without hesitation.

## Scales, and a basket

Something "like scales" falls from Saul's eyes (9:18) — sight restored almost as an afterthought to the deeper reversal already underway. Soon the hunter is smuggled out of Damascus in a basket, lowered over a wall by the very disciples he came to arrest.

## Two miles down the road

The chapter's second half belongs to Peter, quietly: he heals Aeneas at Lydda with a command as plain as a wake-up call — *"Aeneas, Jesus Christ heals you. Get up and make your bed!"* (9:34) — then raises Tabitha at Joppa. No fanfare; entire towns turn "to the Lord" on the strength of it.

## Questions for study

1. What changes if you read persecution of the church as persecution of Jesus himself?
2. Ananias obeyed while still afraid. Is that a lesser obedience than fearless obedience?
3. Why does Luke let Saul's conversion sit next to two comparatively quiet healing stories?
4. Where has God asked you to extend trust to someone whose past record argues against it?
