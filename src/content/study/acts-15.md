The dispute in Acts 15 is not whether Gentiles can be saved — that argument was effectively settled back in Cornelius's living room. It's whether they must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses first, which is a very different and much more practical question: does joining Jesus mean becoming culturally Jewish? Get this wrong and the church either fractures or calcifies into a sect.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible acts 15`

## A yoke nobody could bear

Peter's speech to the council cuts through the debate with a memorable image: *"why do you tempt God, that you should put a yoke on the neck of the disciples which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear? But we believe that we are saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they are"* (15:10-11). Notice the direction of the argument — Jewish believers are saved the same way Gentiles are, not the reverse.

## James's ruling

James, presiding, doesn't demand circumcision but does ask Gentile believers to abstain from a short list tied to idol worship and blood (15:19-20) — enough shared practice to allow Jewish and Gentile believers to eat together, without erasing the line the gospel had just redrawn.

## A split that also bears fruit

The chapter's last scene is almost deflating after such a hard-won unity: Paul and Barnabas disagree sharply over whether to bring John Mark along again, and "the contention grew so sharp that they separated from each other" (15:39). Two flawed men, unable to agree, become two missionary teams instead of one. The church's growth doesn't require its leaders to be perfectly reconciled with each other.

## Questions for study

1. Why is the Gentile-circumcision question more practically dangerous than the question of Gentile salvation itself?
2. What does Peter's "yoke" image say about how heavy religious obligation should be?
3. Does the Paul-Barnabas split in 15:36-41 read as failure, providence, or both?
4. Where might your own community be imposing a "yoke" that isn't actually required?
