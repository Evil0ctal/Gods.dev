Peter opens with advice to wives married to men who don't believe the message — win them "without a word, by the behavior of their wives" (3:1). It's a striking claim: he assumes conduct can accomplish what speech can't, in a household where the theological conversation has presumably already stalled. The chapter keeps returning to this idea that consistent, unhurried goodness carries an argument words have exhausted.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 1peter 3`

## A beauty that doesn't decay

Verses 3-4 contrast outward adornment with "the incorruptible... of a quiet and peaceable spirit," which Peter calls precious in God's sight. He's not banning nice clothes; he's naming what actually lasts versus what fades, in the same vein as chapter 1's "inheritance that doesn't fade" — this is a letter obsessed with what survives time and pressure.

## Suffering for good, not for wrong

By verse 13, Peter widens the lens beyond marriage to suffering generally, and draws a hard line: suffering for doing right is honorable and Christlike, suffering for doing wrong is just consequence. He wants his readers ready to explain their hope "with humility and respect" (3:15) precisely because that hope will be tested by real, undeserved hardship, not hypothetical difficulty.

> *"Always be ready to give an answer to everyone who asks you a reason concerning the hope that is in you."* — 1 Peter 3:15

## Questions for study

1. Where in your life might quiet, consistent conduct do more than an argument would (3:1-2)?
2. How would you answer someone who asked you, right now, for the reason behind your hope (3:15)?
3. Peter distinguishes suffering for doing good from suffering for doing wrong (3:17) — which kind are you currently facing, honestly?
