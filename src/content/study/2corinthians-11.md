Paul calls it foolishness before he even starts, and he's right — the
whole chapter is a resume written backward. Rival teachers ("super
apostles," he calls them with an edge, 11:5) had been impressing Corinth
with polish and confidence. Paul, cornered into the same game, decides to
win it by losing on purpose.

## A serpent in the garden, again

Before the boasting starts, Paul names his real fear: *"I fear, lest by
any means, as the serpent deceived Eve in his craftiness, so your minds
might be corrupted from the simplicity that is in Christ."* (11:3). The
danger isn't heresy shouted from a pulpit; it's subtlety — a slow drift
away from something simple toward something merely impressive.

## The inverted resume

When Paul finally boasts, in verses 23-28, it's a list no one else was
competing for: far more labors, imprisonments, beatings past number,
often near death. Five times thirty-nine lashes. Three shipwrecks, a
night and day adrift. Danger from rivers, robbers, his own countrymen,
Gentiles, false brothers. Hunger, cold, nakedness, sleepless nights. He
piles it up almost breathlessly, then adds, deadpan, "besides those
things that are outside, there is that which presses on me daily, anxiety
for all the assemblies" (11:28) — as if the catalogue of physical
disasters were the easy part.

## The point of the inversion

This isn't self-pity. It's an argument: if credentials are what
Corinth wants, here are Paul's, and they look nothing like triumph. The
real apostle, he's implying, is identifiable by what he's absorbed for
others, not by what he's accumulated for himself.

## Questions for study

1. Where have you been pulled, subtly, from something simple toward
   something merely impressive?
2. If your own hardships were your resume, what would they actually
   prove?
3. What "anxiety for others" presses on you daily, underneath whatever
   else you're dealing with?
