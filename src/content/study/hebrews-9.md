The writer walks through the tabernacle like a tour guide — lampstand, table, showbread in the outer room; behind a veil, the golden altar and the ark in the inner one. What he wants you to notice isn't the furniture. It's the access rules: priests could enter the first room daily, but the second room only the high priest, only once a year, and never without blood.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible hebrews 9`

## Architecture as theology

The layout itself was a sermon, the writer says — the Holy Spirit signifying that the way into the true holy place hadn't yet been opened while the first tabernacle still stood. The building's restrictions were the point, not an inconvenience to work around.

## Blood as the only currency

> *"According to the law, nearly everything is cleansed with blood, and apart from shedding of blood there is no remission."* — Hebrews 9:22

This isn't presented as arbitrary ritual. It's the system's stated price for approaching God, paid annually and never permanently.

## Once, not repeated

Christ enters the greater, true tabernacle "not made with hands" a single time, with his own blood, and the writer contrasts this directly with the yearly repetition that came before.

> *"Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many. To those who wait for him, he will appear a second time, without sin, for salvation."* — Hebrews 9:28 (paraphrased)

## Questions for study

1. What does the tabernacle's restricted access teach about how seriously the old system treated approaching God?
2. Why does something need to be paid for with blood rather than simply forgiven outright, in this framework?
3. What changes for you if Christ's entry into God's presence really was a one-time, finished event rather than an ongoing process?
