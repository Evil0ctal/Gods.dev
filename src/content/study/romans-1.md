Paul has never visited Rome, has no track record with this church, and
still opens with astonishing confidence: "I am not ashamed of the Good
News, for it is the power of God for salvation" (1:16). That's not a
throwaway line — it's the thesis of the whole letter, stated before
the argument even begins. Everything in Romans is Paul unpacking why
that sentence is true.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible romans 1`

## A letter that announces its own outline

Verse 16 and 17 compress the entire book: the gospel is God's power, it
is for everyone — "for the Jew first, and also for the Greek" — and it
reveals "a righteousness of God... from faith to faith." Paul is a
careful architect. He tells you where the building is going before he
lays the first brick.

> *"For I am not ashamed of the Good News of Christ, for it is the power
> of God for salvation for everyone who believes."* — Romans 1:16

## The world Paul actually sees

The chapter's second half (1:18-32) is not a list of other people's sins;
it's a diagnosis of a whole civilization that "knew God" through creation
itself and chose not to honor him as God (1:19-21). What follows —
idolatry, disordered desire, a mind "given up" to what is worthless — is
described as a *consequence*, not merely a crime. Three times the passage
repeats "God gave them up," language of release rather than direct
punishment. The picture is of a rebellion allowed to run its own course.

## Questions for study

1. What would it look like, practically, to be "not ashamed" of something
   you believe when the room isn't friendly to it?
2. Paul says people "knew God" and suppressed that knowledge (1:18-21).
   What's the difference between not knowing and refusing to know?
3. The refrain "God gave them up" appears three times. Does reading
   judgment as *release* rather than *strike* change how you understand
   it?
4. Romans is famous for its structure. What does it tell you about Paul
   that he states his whole argument in the first seventeen verses?
