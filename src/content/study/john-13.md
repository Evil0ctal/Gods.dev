John spends an entire chapter on one action most storytellers would skip: Jesus takes off his outer clothing, wraps a towel around his waist, and washes twelve pairs of feet — including the feet of the man about to betray him. John is careful to tell us Jesus already knew who that was (13:11), and washed his feet anyway.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible john 13`

## Foot-washing as argument

Peter's protest — "You will never wash my feet!" — gets a blunt answer: no washing, no share with Jesus at all. Jesus isn't teaching hygiene; he's teaching what love looks like when it has all authority and nothing to prove (13:3-5). The lesson lands as instruction, not just example: *"you also ought to wash one another's feet"* (13:14).

## The new commandment

Then, after Judas leaves into the night, Jesus gives the disciples something to hold onto:

> *"A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another. Just as I have loved you, you also love one another."* — John 13:34

Not a new idea — love your neighbor is old — but a new measure: *as I have loved you.* That's the whole chapter's foot-washing compressed into a sentence.

## It was night

Judas dips the bread Jesus hands him and walks out. John's narration is spare and devastating: *"having received that morsel, he went out immediately. It was night"* (13:30). One line, and the darkest exit in the book is finished.

## Questions for study

1. What would it cost you, socially, to do for someone what Jesus did for Judas?
2. "As I have loved you" raises the bar on the old command to love your neighbor. How?
3. Why do you think John bothers to note "it was night" the moment Judas leaves?
4. Peter swears loyalty in this chapter and is told he'll deny Jesus three times before dawn (13:38). What does Jesus do with that foreknowledge — condemn Peter, or wash his feet anyway?
