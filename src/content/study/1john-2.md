John writes to "little children" that he's not giving them a new commandment but an old one they've had from the beginning — and then, in the very next breath, calls it new, because it's true "in him and in you" as the darkness passes and the true light already shines (2:7-8). He seems unbothered by the apparent contradiction. The command to love isn't new in content, but it's new in every moment someone actually obeys it for the first time.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 1john 2`

## Loving the brother as the visible test

Verses 9-11 make the test uncomfortably concrete: claiming to be in the light while hating a brother puts you still in darkness, while loving your brother is what keeps you walking in light with nothing to make you stumble. John keeps refusing to let "light" and "darkness" stay abstract categories — they cash out immediately in how you treat the person in front of you.

## Antichrists already among you

By verse 18, John names a present danger rather than a future one — "even now many antichrists have arisen," people who went out from the community because they were never truly part of it (2:19). This isn't alarmism; it's diagnosis. Departure itself becomes evidence, retroactively clarifying who belonged in the first place.

> *"He who says he is in the light and hates his brother is in the darkness even until now."* — 1 John 2:9

## Questions for study

1. How can the command to love be both old and new at the same time, according to verses 7-8?
2. Where does the test in verses 9-11 — love or hatred toward a brother — expose something in your own current relationships?
3. What does verse 19's logic ("they went out from us, but they were not of us") suggest about how to interpret people leaving a faith community?
