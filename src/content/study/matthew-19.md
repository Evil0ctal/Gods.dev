The Pharisees open with a trap question — is divorce lawful "for any cause" (19:3) — and Jesus answers by going back past Moses to the beginning: *"Haven't you read that he who made them from the beginning made them male and female... So then they are no more two, but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart"* (19:4-6). He's asked about a legal loophole and answers with a picture of marriage as a made thing, not a negotiable contract.

## A picture, then a permission

Verses 8-9 concede that Moses allowed divorce "because of the hardness of your hearts" — a permission granted to a broken situation, not the original design. Jesus keeps both truths in view: the ideal stands, and human hardness still gets legislated for. It's a chapter that resists being flattened into a single rule.

## The disciples who wanted quiet

When children are brought to be blessed, the disciples try to shoo them off (19:13). Jesus reverses it flatly: *"Allow the little children to come to me, and don't forbid them, for the Kingdom of Heaven belongs to ones like these"* (19:14). The disciples treat children as an interruption to important work. Jesus treats them as the point.

## The one thing he couldn't sell

A rich young man asks what he lacks, and Jesus names the one asset he won't part with: sell what you have and follow me. He "went away sad, for he was one who had great possessions" (19:22). The disciples are stunned — if the blessed and successful can't get in, who can? Jesus' answer refuses despair: *"With men this is impossible, but with God all things are possible"* (19:26).

## Questions for study

1. Where do you tend to treat Jesus' teaching as either pure rule or pure exception — and where does this chapter resist both?
2. What would it look like to stop treating "lesser" people or tasks as interruptions?
3. What is the "great possession" you'd walk away sad rather than release?
4. Verse 26 is often quoted for miracles — how does it apply to something as ordinary as generosity?
