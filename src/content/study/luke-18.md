Luke gives away the point of the parable in advance: Jesus told it so that his hearers would always pray and not give up. The judge in the story fears neither God nor man and admits it openly — he only relents because the widow's persistence has become more exhausting than granting her request. It's a strange way to teach about prayer, arguing from a bad judge's laziness to God's justice, but the logic runs: if even that gets results, how much more a Father who actually wants to answer.

## Two men, two postures

A Pharisee thanks God that he isn't like other men, listing his own merits; a tax collector won't even lift his eyes, beating his chest, asking only for mercy. Jesus says the tax collector went home justified — the one with nothing to point to except his own need.

## What still lacks

A rich ruler has kept the commandments since his youth and asks what else there is; Jesus tells him to sell everything and follow him, and he leaves grieving. Right after, Peter points out that the disciples have already given up everything — and Jesus doesn't dispute it, he promises it isn't wasted.

> *"God, be merciful to me, a sinner!"* — Luke 18:13

> *"The things which are impossible with men are possible with God."* — Luke 18:27

## Questions for study

1. Why base a lesson on persistence in prayer on an unjust judge instead of a good one?
2. What separates the Pharisee's prayer from the tax collector's, beyond just the words used?
3. Why does keeping every commandment since youth still leave the rich ruler short of something?
4. What is the "one thing" you still lack, and what does it cost to name it?
