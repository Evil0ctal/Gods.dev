Someone in the crowd wants Jesus to settle a family inheritance argument. He declines the role of judge and instead tells a story: a rich man's land produces so well he tears down his barns to build bigger ones, plans years of ease, and dies that same night, still admiring his own plans. "Whose will they be?" God asks him, and the story just ends there, unanswered.

## Ravens don't have barns

From there Jesus moves to birds that don't sow or reap and are still fed, and flowers that don't spin thread and are still dressed better than Solomon. The logic isn't that worry is pointless because things work out — it's that a life built on hoarding misreads what actually sustains it.

## Treasure and where it points

"Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also" isn't a warning about greed alone; it's a statement about direction. Whatever you store up ends up steering you, whether you notice or not.

## Fire and an unsettled account

The chapter turns sharply toward urgency — division, not automatic peace; reading the sky better than reading the moment; settling with an accuser before you're dragged to court. The barns-and-ease posture of the rich fool gets no room here.

> *"Beware! Keep yourselves from covetousness, for a man's life doesn't consist of the abundance of the things which he possesses."* — Luke 12:15

> *"Where your treasure is, there will your heart be also."* — Luke 12:34

## Questions for study

1. Why does Jesus refuse to arbitrate the inheritance dispute directly?
2. What is the rich fool's actual mistake — is it wealth itself, or something else?
3. How does "where your treasure is" work as a diagnostic rather than just a warning?
4. What would it look like to "read" the present moment as carefully as you read the weather?
