Seventy — or seventy-two, depending on the manuscript tradition behind your translation — get sent out ahead of Jesus with instructions to travel without extra sandals or money and to pronounce peace on whatever house receives them. It's a bigger, more vulnerable operation than the sending of the twelve back in chapter nine, and it works: they come back amazed that even demons submit to them in Jesus's name.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 10`

## Rejoice in the right thing

Jesus redirects their excitement. Don't celebrate that spirits obey you, he says — celebrate that your names are written in heaven. It's a small but pointed correction: don't let the thrill of spiritual power become the thing you build your identity on.

## Who is my neighbor, exactly

A legal expert tries to justify himself by asking Jesus to define "neighbor" narrowly. Jesus answers with a Samaritan — a member of a group his audience despised — who stops for a beaten stranger that a priest and a Levite had already passed by. The question gets flipped: not who qualifies as my neighbor, but who acted like one.

## Martha, Mary, and one necessary thing

At Bethany, Martha is busy with the work of hospitality while Mary sits and listens. Jesus doesn't scold Martha for serving — he tells her she's anxious about too many things, when only one is necessary.

> *"Don't rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven."* — Luke 10:20

> *"Go and do likewise."* — Luke 10:37

## Questions for study

1. Why does Jesus redirect the seventy's joy away from their power over demons?
2. Why answer "who is my neighbor" with a story instead of a definition?
3. What does the Samaritan risk by stopping that the priest and Levite avoid?
4. Is Martha's problem that she serves, or something else?
