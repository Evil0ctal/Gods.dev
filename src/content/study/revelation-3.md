Sardis has a reputation, and Jesus opens by naming the gap between it and the truth: "you have a name that you are alive, but you are dead" (v.1). It's possibly the most uncomfortable line in all seven letters, because it isn't about persecution or false teaching — it's about a church coasting on a memory of itself.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible revelation 3`

## Sardis: alive in name only

No specific heresy gets named here, no outside threat. The problem is simply drift, unnoticed until Jesus says it out loud.

## Philadelphia: a door held open

Philadelphia, by contrast, gets no rebuke and one of the warmest promises in the letters — an open door nothing can shut, given to a church with "little power" but real faithfulness.

> *"I have set before you an open door, which no one can shut."* — Revelation 3:8

## Laodicea: neither hot nor cold

The most famous line in this chapter is aimed at a church, not at outsiders on the fence about faith — self-satisfied, materially secure, and utterly unaware of its own poverty.

> *"You say, 'I am rich, and have gotten riches, and have need of nothing,' and don't know that you are the wretched one, miserable, poor, blind, and naked."* — Revelation 3:17

Even here, though, the chapter ends with an invitation, not a closing door — Jesus still knocking, still asking to be let in.

## Questions for study

1. What's the danger in a church, or a person, having a reputation for being alive that no longer matches reality?
2. Why might Philadelphia, described as having "little power," receive the strongest promise of the three churches here?
3. The "lukewarm" image is often used on non-believers, but it's addressed to a church. Does that change how you read it?
