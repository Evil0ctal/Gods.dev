The Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5-7) is three chapters of the most
famous words ever spoken: the Beatitudes, salt and light, turn the other
cheek, love your enemies, the Lord's Prayer, do not worry, judge not, the
golden rule, the house on the rock. If you have only heard one sermon in
your life, fragments of this one are probably in it.

> Start with the opening: `bible matthew 5:3-12` — then take a chapter at
> a time: `bible matthew 5`, `bible matthew 6`, `bible matthew 7`.

## Congratulations to the wrong people

The sermon opens with eight "blessed are" statements (5:3-12). The Greek
word *makarios* is closer to "congratulations" than to a vague spiritual
glow. Hear it that way and the shock returns:

*Congratulations to the poor in spirit. Congratulations to those who
mourn. Congratulations to the meek.*

Every culture, ancient and modern, congratulates the opposite people: the
confident, the winning, the untouchable. Jesus opens his manifesto by
flipping the scoreboard. The Beatitudes are not eight demands — be
meeker! mourn harder! — they are eight announcements that the kingdom's
economy runs on a different currency, and the people at the bottom of the
world's table are at the head of this one.

## "You have heard... but I tell you"

Six times in chapter 5, Jesus quotes the received tradition and then goes
*further*: anger is the root of murder (5:21-22), contempt is the root of
adultery (5:27-28), love must extend to enemies (5:43-44). He is not
lowering the bar of the law; he is tracing every command back to the
heart it was always aiming at.

This is worth sitting with, because the sermon is often misread as
"niceness." It is far more radical: it relocates morality from behavior
management to heart surgery.

## The quiet religion of chapter 6

Chapter 6 targets performance: giving, praying, and fasting done "to be
seen by others." The prescription is secrecy — a closed door, an
unadvertised gift (6:3-6). At the center sits the Lord's Prayer
(`bible matthew 6:9-13`), fifty-some words that begin not with a request
but with a reorientation: *your kingdom come, your will be done.*

The chapter ends with the anxiety passage (6:25-34) — birds, lilies, and
the most repeated command in the Bible: *do not be afraid.* Note that the
argument against worry is not "everything will go your way." It is that
you have a Father who sees, and tomorrow's trouble is tomorrow's (6:34 —
possibly the most practical verse in scripture).

## Two houses, one storm

The sermon closes with a builder's parable (7:24-27). Two houses, and in
the story the storm hits *both*. The difference is not that one builder
avoided the weather; it is what the house was standing on when the
weather came. Jesus' final claim is quietly enormous: the stable
foundation is not good ideas in general — it is *"these words of mine,
and does them."*

Hearing without doing, he says, is sand.

## Questions for study

1. Rewrite two or three Beatitudes in your own words for your own
   context. Who are the "poor in spirit" where you live and work?
2. Which "you have heard... but I tell you" escalation lands closest to
   home for you?
3. What is one practice from chapter 6 you could do in genuine secrecy
   this week — known to no one?
4. Storms in the final parable are a given, not a punishment. How does
   that reframe whatever storm you are in?
