Luke opens like no other Gospel — not with a genealogy, not with a cosmic prologue, but with an actual preface addressed to a named reader, explaining his method. He says he investigated things carefully and wrote "in order." That framing matters for how the rest of the book reads: less a collection of sayings, more a constructed account with a shape and an argument.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 1`

## A historian's preface
Luke tells you upfront what he's doing.

> *"Having traced the course of all things accurately from the first, to write to you in order."* — Luke 1:3

## The great reversal
From Mary's song in chapter one — the proud scattered, the hungry filled, the rich sent away empty — Luke keeps returning to a world turned upside down. His Gospel gives more space than the others to women, to the poor, to Samaritans, to tax collectors, to a Gentile centurion's faith. Jesus announces his own mission in his hometown synagogue with a line from Isaiah that sets the whole book's agenda.

> *"The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he anointed me to preach good news to the poor."* — Luke 4:18

## The long road to Jerusalem
A large middle section of Luke, roughly chapters 9 through 19, is structured as one long journey toward Jerusalem, packed with parables found nowhere else: the good Samaritan, the prodigal son, the rich man and Lazarus. The road itself becomes the teaching space, right up until the triumphal entry, the trials, the cross, and a resurrection that sends the disciples out to keep going where Luke's second volume, Acts, picks up the story.

## Questions for study
1. Why might Luke have chosen to open with a formal historical preface rather than a genealogy or poem?
2. What pattern of reversal shows up repeatedly across the book, and why does it matter to Luke's argument?
3. How does the "journey to Jerusalem" structure shape the parables found only in this Gospel?
4. Which reversal in Luke, of status, wealth, or expectation, challenges you most directly?
