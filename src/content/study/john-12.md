Six days before Passover, Mary takes a pound of expensive nard — worth close to a year's wages — and pours it on Jesus' feet, wiping it with her hair. The house fills with the smell (12:3). Judas objects on financial grounds; John, not shy about motives, tells us Judas kept the money box and used to help himself to what was put into it (12:6). The contrast is the chapter in miniature: extravagant love beside calculating suspicion.

## Two crowds, one week

The next day a different crowd throngs the road into Jerusalem waving palm branches: *"Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, the King of Israel!"* (12:13). Triumph and burial-anointing sit one paragraph apart — John wants you to feel both at once, because Jesus does.

## A grain of wheat

When some Greeks ask to see him, Jesus answers with an image instead of an audience:

> *"Most certainly, I tell you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains by itself alone. But if it dies, it bears much fruit."* — John 12:24

Glory, in this Gospel, keeps arriving disguised as a funeral.

## Light while you have it

The chapter closes with Jesus pleading, almost urgently: *"Walk while you have the light, that darkness doesn't overtake you"* (12:35). John notes that many still wouldn't believe, even after all the signs — belief, it turns out, isn't simply a shortage of evidence.

## Questions for study

1. Judas's complaint about the perfume sounded practical. What makes a "reasonable" objection actually a cover for something else?
2. What would it cost you to give as extravagantly as Mary did?
3. The grain-of-wheat saying reframes death as fruitfulness. Where in your life does that principle apply right now?
4. Why does John pair triumph and foreboding so closely in this chapter?
