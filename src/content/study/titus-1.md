Paul does something unusual in verse 12 — quotes a Cretan poet insulting Cretans: "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, and idle gluttons" — and then agrees: "this testimony is true" (1:12-13). It's a blunt way to open a letter about qualifications for leaders, but it sets the stakes: Titus is cleaning up churches in a culture with a reputation problem, using leaders drawn from that same culture.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible titus 1`

## Appointed, not just gifted

Paul's instruction to Titus is specific: "appoint elders in every city, as I directed you" (1:5) — structural work, not just spiritual encouragement. The qualifications that follow (1:6-9) are almost entirely about ordinary reputation: not accused of wildness, not quick-tempered, hospitable, self-controlled. Nothing here requires charisma. Everything here requires consistency.

## Mouths that need stopping

Against those qualified leaders, Paul sets "unruly men, vain talkers and deceivers" who teach "for dishonest gain's sake" and whose "mouths must be stopped" (1:10-11). The contrast is deliberate: sound leaders are described by character; false teachers are described by motive — money.

## Purity is internal, not circumstantial

The chapter's closing line reframes the whole problem: "to the pure, all things are pure, but to those who are defiled and unbelieving, nothing is pure... both their mind and their conscience are defiled" (1:15). Corruption, in Paul's diagnosis, isn't first about what you touch. It's about what has already touched you.

## Questions for study

1. What do you make of Paul agreeing with a harsh cultural stereotype (1:12-13) instead of dismissing it?
2. Look at the elder qualifications in 1:6-9 — how many are about skill versus ordinary character?
3. "Mouths must be stopped" (1:11) because of "dishonest gain" — where does money corrupt teaching in ways that are easy to miss?
4. How does 1:15 change where you'd look for the source of corruption — outside you, or in you?
