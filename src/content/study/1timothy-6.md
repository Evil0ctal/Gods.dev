Get the famous line right before anything else: Paul does not say money is the root of all evil. He says "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (6:10) — a claim about the heart's attachment, not about currency itself, and the difference changes how the rest of the chapter should be read.

## Contentment as combat readiness

Between the money warning and its bookends sits a working definition of enough: "having food and clothing, we will be content with that" (6:8). Paul frames the alternative — wanting to be rich — not as ambition but as a trap with teeth: "a temptation, a snare... foolish and harmful lusts" (6:9). Wealth itself isn't condemned here. The falling-in is.

## The good confession

Verse 12 tells Timothy to "fight the good fight of faith" and take hold of the life he confessed "in the sight of many witnesses," then Paul anchors that confession to Christ's own, made "before Pontius Pilate" (6:13). Two confessions, one public and one under threat of death, bracket the charge. Timothy's faith is asked to look like Jesus' faith: costly, and spoken aloud regardless of the room.

## A different charge to the rich

Verses 17-19 aren't a condemnation of wealth but a redirection of it — hope moved off "the uncertainty of riches" and onto God, riches turned into generosity, "laying up... a good foundation" (6:19). A chapter that opened with slaves serving masters ends with the wealthy serving everyone else. Status isn't the problem in Paul's mind. What you do with it is.

## Questions for study

1. Reread 6:10 carefully. What changes if you've been misquoting it as "money is the root of all evil"?
2. What's the difference, in your own life, between providing for yourself and "determined to be rich" (6:9)?
3. Why link Timothy's public confession to Jesus' confession before Pilate (6:12-13)?
4. What would it look like to be "rich in good works" (6:18) rather than merely rich?
