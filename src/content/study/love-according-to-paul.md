*"Love is patient and is kind..."* — you have heard it at weddings,
printed on canvas, lettered over kitchen doorways. Which is fine, except
that 1 Corinthians 13 was not written for a wedding. It was written to
the most dysfunctional church in the New Testament.

> Read the whole chapter: `bible 1 john 4:7-12` pairs well after it, but
> start here — `bible 1corinthians 13`

## The context: a church at war

Corinth's church was split into celebrity factions (chapter 1), suing
each other in court (chapter 6), shaming the poor at communal meals
(chapter 11), and ranking each other by spiritual gifts (chapters 12 and
14). Chapter 13 sits deliberately between the two chapters about
spiritual gifts. It is not a break from the argument; it is the argument.

Paul's point lands harder when you know the audience: *you can be
impressive and useless at the same time.*

## Without love, noise

The chapter opens with an audit (13:1-3). Tongues of men and angels?
Without love: a gong. Prophecy, knowledge, mountain-moving faith? Without
love: *"I am nothing."* Giving away everything, even martyrdom? Without
love: *"it profits me nothing."*

Notice the escalation — Paul lists the most spectacular spiritual résumé
imaginable and zeroes it out. In an age that measures accounts by
followers and output, verse 2 remains one of the most subversive
sentences ever written: **it is possible to be extraordinary and be
nothing.**

## Love does

Verses 4-7 are the famous ones, and in Greek they are not adjectives but
*verbs*. Love isn't patient as a personality trait; love *practices
patience*. Fifteen verbs, and every one of them is observable behavior:

- suffers long, is kind
- doesn't envy, doesn't brag, is not proud
- doesn't behave inappropriately, doesn't seek its own way
- is not provoked, keeps no ledger of wrongs
- doesn't rejoice in unrighteousness, rejoices with the truth
- bears, believes, hopes, endures — *all things*

A useful exercise, older than any of us: read 13:4-7 and replace the word
"love" with your own name. See how far you get before wincing. Then
notice that the early church read it with a different substitution in
mind — the life of Jesus fits every verb without strain.

## The things that last

The chapter ends by sorting everything into two piles: what expires and
what remains (13:8-13). Prophecies, tongues, knowledge — scaffolding, all
of it, coming down when the building is done. *"Now we see in a mirror,
dimly, but then face to face."*

Three things survive the demolition: faith, hope, love. And of the three
survivors, one wears the crown: *"the greatest of these is love."* Not
because faith and hope are small — but because in the end, faith becomes
sight and hope becomes arrival, while love simply continues. Love is the
only thing you practice now that you will still be doing then.

## Questions for study

1. Where in your life are you currently "a resounding gong" — impressive
   output, low love?
2. Pick one verb from 13:4-7 to practice deliberately for a week — the
   one that made you wince. What would it look like on Tuesday afternoon?
3. "Keeps no record of wrongs" — what ledger are you still maintaining,
   and what would it cost to close it?
4. Read chapter 12 and 14 around it. How does chapter 13 change what
   "being gifted" means?
