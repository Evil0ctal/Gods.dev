The disciples ask Jesus who is greatest in the kingdom of heaven, and instead of ranking them, he calls a child over and stands it in the middle of the circle (18:2). No speech about humility first — the object lesson comes before the explanation. Children in that world had no status to leverage, nothing to offer, nothing to prove. That, Jesus says, is the posture that gets you in the door at all.

## Whoever causes one of these to stumble

The tone turns severe fast. Millstones, cutting off hands, plucking out eyes (18:6-9) — deliberately violent language about how seriously Jesus takes harm done to "little ones," whether children or the vulnerable in the faith. It's not a chapter about self-mutilation; it's a chapter about how far he expects you to go to avoid wrecking someone weaker than you.

## When your brother sins against you

The process in 18:15-17 — go to them privately, then with witnesses, then to the church — is often quoted as a discipline manual and less often noticed as a *restoration* manual. Every step aims at winning the person back, not winning the argument. The goal named up front is "you have gained your brother," not "you have proven your point."

## The debt too large to repay

Peter asks how many times to forgive and offers seven as a generous number. Jesus answers "seventy times seven" (18:22) and tells a parable about a servant forgiven an impossible debt who then chokes a fellow servant over pocket change (18:23-35). The math is the whole sermon: whatever's owed you is trivial next to what you've been let off.

## Questions for study

1. What would it cost you, practically, to treat someone powerless the way Jesus treats the child in verse 2?
2. Which step of 18:15-17 do you tend to skip — going privately, or involving others?
3. Is there a debt you're still holding over someone that, honestly measured, is small?
4. "Seventy times seven" isn't a number to count to — what is it actually asking of you?
