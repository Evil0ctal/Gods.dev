John's Gospel builds its case for who Jesus is through a series of blunt self-descriptions, each opening with the same two words: "I am." In Greek that phrase, *ego eimi*, carries a weight an English reader can miss — it echoes the language God uses of himself at the burning bush. John spreads seven of these across nine chapters, and together they form less a list than an argument.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible john 6`

## The claims, in order

Jesus calls himself the bread of life (6:35), the light of the world (8:12), the door of the sheep (10:9), the good shepherd (10:11), the resurrection and the life (11:25), the way, the truth, and the life (14:6), and the true vine (15:1). Each image answers a different human need — hunger, darkness, safety, death, direction, fruitfulness — and each points back to the same person.

## Not abstractions, occasions

None of these sayings arrives as detached theology. The bread of life follows a miraculous feeding; the good shepherd follows a controversy about a healed blind man expelled from the synagogue; the resurrection and the life is spoken standing at a friend's tomb. John ties the claims to concrete moments, which is its own argument: this is not philosophy, it's a person responding to real need.

> *"I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies."* — John 11:25

## Questions for study

1. Which of the seven images meets you where you actually are right now?
2. Why might John have chosen concrete, everyday images — bread, light, a door — rather than abstract titles?
3. What does the timing of each saying, tied to a specific event, add to its meaning?
4. Read the burning bush scene in Exodus 3. What changes when you hear "I am" against that background?
