A woman breaks open a jar of pure nard worth a year's wages and pours it on Jesus's head, and the first response in the room is outrage at the waste (14:4-5). Jesus's defense reframes the act entirely: "She has done what she could. She has anointed my body beforehand for the burying" (14:8). Nobody else in the room, including disciples who've just heard three passion predictions, seems to understand what's about to happen. She does.

## Bread and a cup, reinterpreted

At the Passover meal, Jesus takes the oldest ritual in Israel's story and rewrites its center: bread becomes his body, the cup his blood "of the covenant, which is poured out for many" (14:22-24). He institutes a new memory inside an old one, without asking permission from the tradition he's reshaping.

## The prayer that doesn't get an easy answer

In Gethsemane, Jesus asks, honestly, for the cup to be removed — and then submits anyway:

> *"He said, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible to you. Please remove this cup from me. However, not what I desire, but what you desire.'"* — Mark 14:36

The disciples, meanwhile, can't stay awake even for an hour (14:37), a small human failure set right next to the largest moment of Jesus's resolve.

## A denial, predicted and fulfilled

By the chapter's end, Peter — loud in his loyalty a few hours earlier — denies knowing Jesus three times before a rooster crows, exactly as predicted, and breaks down weeping (14:72). Mark doesn't soften the fall for its most prominent disciple.

## Questions for study

1. The woman's extravagance looks like waste to everyone except Jesus. What "wasteful" devotion have you second-guessed in yourself or others?
2. "Not what I desire, but what you desire" — where in your own life is that prayer currently unanswered, or unresolved?
3. Why do you think Mark records the disciples' sleep in such unflattering detail?
4. Peter's collapse is total, and so is his eventual restoration elsewhere in the Gospels. Does knowing the ending change how you read his denial here?
