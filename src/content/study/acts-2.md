The sound comes first — "a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind" filling the whole house — and then the sight: something like tongues of fire resting on each of them (2:2-3). Whatever this is, it isn't subtle. Jerusalem is packed with pilgrims from across the empire for the festival, and suddenly Galilean fishermen are being understood, each in his own native language (2:6-8).

## An old promise, freshly kept

Peter doesn't reach for a new argument to explain the chaos; he reaches for the prophet Joel: "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (2:17). The point of his sermon isn't that something unprecedented has happened — it's that something long-promised finally has.

## Cut to the heart

Peter's sermon builds to an accusation the crowd can't deflect — they had a hand in crucifying "this Jesus, whom God has raised up" (2:32, 36). The response is immediate and physical: *"they were cut to the heart, and said... 'Brothers, what shall we do?'"* (2:37). Conviction, here, isn't guilt for its own sake; it's the doorway to the next question.

## A community, not just an event

The chapter's famous ending describes what three thousand new believers actually did next: they devoted themselves "to the apostles' teaching and fellowship, to the breaking of bread, and prayer," sharing possessions so that no one went without (2:42-45). Pentecost wasn't a spectacle that faded; it produced a way of life.

## Questions for study

1. Peter's answer to the crowd's confusion is a Scripture they already knew, not a novel claim. What does that suggest about how to explain the unexpected?
2. What does it look like, practically, to be "cut to the heart" rather than merely informed?
3. Acts 2:42-45 describes devotion to teaching, fellowship, breaking bread, and prayer. Which of those four is weakest in your own life right now?
4. Why might sharing possessions have been the natural next step after Pentecost, rather than an add-on?
