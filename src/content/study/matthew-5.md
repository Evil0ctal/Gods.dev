Jesus sits down on a mountainside — the posture of a teacher with authority, not a casual speaker — and opens with a list of people you would not normally congratulate: the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, the hungry, the persecuted. Every one of these is a category most societies try to escape or hide. Jesus opens his most famous sermon by calling them blessed, and he means it as fact, not consolation prize.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible matthew 5`

## Salt that has lost its usefulness

After the Beatitudes, Jesus tells his hearers they are salt and light — not that they should try to become these things, but that they already are, with a warning attached: salt that loses its flavor is good for nothing but being thrown out (5:13). Identity here carries responsibility. You don't hide a city on a hill; you don't cover a lamp.

> *"Even so, let your light shine before men; that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven."* — Matthew 5:16

## Raising the bar, not lowering it

The back half of the chapter is a series of "you have heard it said... but I tell you" statements. Jesus doesn't relax the Law's demands on murder, adultery, oaths, and retaliation — he traces each one back to its root in the heart. Anger is murder's seed. Contempt is adultery's seed. He ends the chapter with the hardest line in the sermon: "You therefore shall be perfect, even as your Father in heaven is perfect" (5:48) — not a standard anyone reaches by trying harder, but one that reframes the whole project as grace-shaped, not rule-shaped.

## Questions for study

1. Which Beatitude describes a state you have actually experienced, and how did it feel to hear it called "blessed"?
2. Jesus relocates morality from actions to the heart behind them. Where does that reframing convict you most directly?
3. What would it look like this week to be "salt" in a specific relationship or room, rather than blending in?
