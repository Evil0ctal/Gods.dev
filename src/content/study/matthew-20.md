A landowner hires workers throughout the day — some at dawn, some with only an hour of daylight left — and pays every one of them the same wage (20:1-16). The all-day workers do the math and object, reasonably, by any normal accounting. The owner's answer isn't an apology: *"Isn't it lawful for me to do what I want to with what I own? Or is your eye evil, because I am good?"* (20:15). The parable isn't about labor law. It's about what happens when generosity meets people keeping score.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible matthew 20`

## The math that grace refuses

"Envy" in the Greek is literally an "evil eye" — a way of seeing that turns someone else's gift into your personal loss. The parable exposes how naturally we convert someone else's grace into our own grievance, as if mercy were a fixed pie.

## A request dressed as a favor

James and John's mother asks for the best seats in the kingdom for her sons (20:20-21). Jesus doesn't rebuke the ambition so much as redirect it: *"Whoever desires to become great among you shall be your servant"* (20:26). Greatness in his kingdom is not withheld from ambitious people — it's redefined for them.

## The blind men who wouldn't be quieted

Two blind men shout for mercy near Jericho, and the crowd tells them to shut up (20:31). They shout louder. Jesus stops for them anyway — the third time in a few verses that the "wrong" request gets an answer, because *"the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many"* (20:28).

## Questions for study

1. When has someone else's blessing felt like your loss? What did that reveal about your own scorekeeping?
2. James and John's mother wanted status for her sons. What ambition of yours needs the same redirection, not rebuke?
3. The crowd tried to silence the blind men. Whose voice have you helped silence, intentionally or not?
4. Verse 28 defines greatness as service. What's one place this week you could actually apply it?
