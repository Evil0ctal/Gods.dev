Something had gone badly wrong between Paul and Corinth by the time this
letter opens. There had been a painful visit (2:1), a severe letter
written "out of much affliction and anguish of heart... with many tears"
(2:4), and real fear that the relationship might not survive it. Titus
finally brings word that it has — the church repented, and Paul writes
this letter partly out of relief, partly to finish an argument he'd
rather not have had to make: that his authority is real precisely
because it doesn't look impressive.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2corinthians 1`

## Comfort, then collection

Chapters 1-7 are the most personal writing in Paul's letters — comfort
in affliction (1:3-11), the two kinds of sorrow (7:10), ministers as
"treasure in jars of clay" (4:7), and the pivot verse of the whole book:
"if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (5:17). Chapters 8-9
change register entirely into practical fundraising for the Jerusalem
church, built on one theological hinge: Christ "though he was rich...
became poor" (8:9), so giving imitates rather than merely funds.

## The fight nobody wanted

Chapters 10-13 turn combative. Rival teachers ("super-apostles," 11:5)
had been outshining Paul on style and confidence, and he's forced into a
"fool's speech" — boasting not in accomplishments but in beatings,
shipwrecks, and sleepless nights (11:23-28). It lands on the letter's
real thesis, delivered as a divine answer to an unanswered prayer:

*"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in
weakness."* — 2 Corinthians 12:9

## One argument, two halves

Read straight through, the letter has an odd shape — warm reconciliation
giving way to sharp confrontation — but one argument runs underneath both
halves: legitimate ministry is proven by what it absorbs for others, not
by what it displays for itself. That argument required Paul's own
credibility as evidence, which is why the letter gets so personal.

## Questions for study

1. Where do you evaluate leadership — including your own — by polish
   rather than by what it has absorbed for others?
2. Read 7:10 and 12:9 side by side. How do they describe two different
   kinds of "necessary pain"?
3. What would it cost you to lead, give, or serve using scars rather
   than credentials as your case?
