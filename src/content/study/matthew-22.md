A king prepares a wedding feast for his son, and the invited guests don't just decline — they ignore the invitation and go back to their farms and businesses, some even killing the messengers (22:2-6). The king's response is to fill the hall with whoever will come, "both bad and good," pulled in from the streets (22:9-10). It's a parable about an invitation so good that refusing it looks, on its face, insane — and people refuse it anyway, for the smallest of reasons.

## A coin with the wrong face on it

The religious leaders try to trap Jesus on Roman taxation — pay or don't pay, either answer looks bad. He asks for a coin, points at Caesar's image stamped on it, and says: *"Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's"* (22:21). The line dodges the trap by asking a better question than the one they posed: what bears whose image, and what do you actually owe each?

## A question with no comeback

The Sadducees try a hypothetical about levirate marriage in the resurrection; Jesus corrects their premise about resurrected life entirely (22:23-33). Then a Pharisee asks which commandment is greatest, and gets an answer that collapses the whole law into two lines: *"You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart... You shall love your neighbor as yourself. On these two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets"* (22:37-40).

## Questions for study

1. What "farm" or "business" of your own routinely wins out over a better invitation?
2. Caesar's coin bears Caesar's image — what in your life bears God's image, and what do you actually render to it?
3. How does love of God and love of neighbor function as a hinge rather than just two more items on a list?
4. Who's currently uninvited from your table that the king in this parable would have gone looking for?
