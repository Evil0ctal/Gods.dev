Nobody comes off well in this chapter except the gospel itself. Paul
describes going up to Jerusalem, getting his mission to the Gentiles
confirmed by the leading apostles — then turning around and publicly
opposing one of them. "When Peter came to Antioch, I resisted him to his
face, because he stood condemned" (2:11). This is not a private
disagreement smoothed over later; Paul tells us he did it in front of
everyone.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible galatians 2`

## The offense was small; the stakes weren't

What triggered it looks minor on the surface: Peter had been eating with
Gentile believers, then quietly stopped when certain men arrived "from
James," fearing "those of the circumcision" (2:12). No sermon was
preached, no doctrine officially changed. Peter just started sitting at
a different table. Paul treats that small, social retreat as a direct
threat to the gospel's truth, because it implied — without saying so —
that Gentile believers were second-class unless they conformed further.

## Crucified with Christ

Out of this confrontation comes one of Paul's most quoted lines,
delivered as the theological payoff of the whole argument:

*"I have been crucified with Christ, and it is no longer I who live,
but Christ lives in me. That life which I now live in the flesh, I live
by faith in the Son of God, who loved me, and gave himself up for me."*
— Galatians 2:20

## The stakes named plainly

Paul closes the chapter with the sentence that makes the whole
confrontation make sense: "if righteousness is through the law, then
Christ died for nothing" (2:21). If keeping the rules could have gotten
anyone there, the cross would have been an unnecessary detour.

## Questions for study

1. Why might a small social gesture — where someone sits, who they eat
   with — carry theological weight?
2. Have you ever had to confront someone respected publicly because the
   private cost of silence was too high?
3. What would change in your life if you took 2:20 as a literal
   description rather than a slogan?
