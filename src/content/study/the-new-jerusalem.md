Notice the direction of travel. Popular imagination pictures the end of the story as souls floating up to heaven, but John sees the opposite: *"I saw the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God"* (21:2). The final hope of Scripture isn't escape from earth — it's heaven relocating to earth, remade.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible revelation 21`

## What's missing is the point

John describes the new creation largely by what's absent from it. "The sea is no more" (21:1) — for readers who experienced the sea as chaos and threat, that's relief. And then, more directly:

> *"He will wipe away every tear from their eyes. Death will be no more; neither will there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain, any more."* — Revelation 21:4

Grief isn't managed here or explained away. It's ended.

## A city with an open gate

The new Jerusalem is described with almost absurd precision — measurements, gemstones, twelve gates of pearl. But one detail stands out more than the dimensions: the gates "will in no way be shut by day, for there will be no night there" (21:25). A city that never has to lock its doors is a city that has run out of threats to keep out.

## The last invitation

The book that opens with judgment closes with an open-ended offer: "he who is thirsty, let him come. He who desires, let him take the water of life freely" (22:17). Even in its final lines, Revelation is still handing out invitations, not just verdicts.

## Questions for study

1. Why do you think the vision emphasizes a city coming down, rather than souls going up?
2. What do you make of the specific things named as absent: sea, tears, death, night?
3. What would it look like to live now in a way that anticipates a world with no need for locked gates?
4. How does the invitation in 22:17 sit alongside the warnings elsewhere in Revelation?
