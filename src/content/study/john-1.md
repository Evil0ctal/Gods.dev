Matthew starts with a family tree. Luke starts with a preface and a pregnancy. John starts before any of that, before time itself, with the Word already existing alongside God and being God. It's a jarring opening if you've just come from the other three Gospels, and it's clearly deliberate: John wants you to know who this is before he tells you what he did.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible john 1`

## Before the beginning, then flesh
The prologue moves fast, from eternity to incarnation in a handful of lines.

> *"The Word became flesh, and lived among us."* — John 1:14

## A finger pointing away from itself
John the Baptist gets a very different role here than he does elsewhere. He isn't primarily a preacher of repentance in this Gospel — he's a witness whose entire job is to point at someone else and get out of the way.

## Come and see
The chapter ends with a string of ordinary recruitments — Andrew, Peter, Philip, and finally Nathanael, who is openly skeptical that anything good could come from Nazareth. Philip doesn't argue him out of his skepticism.

> *"Philip said to him, 'Come and see.'"* — John 1:46

That answer, not a rebuttal but an invitation, sets the pattern for how this whole Gospel wants to be approached.

## Questions for study
1. Why might John choose to open with eternity rather than a birth narrative?
2. What does it mean that John the Baptist's role here is defined almost entirely as testimony?
3. Why does Philip answer Nathanael's skepticism with an invitation instead of an argument?
4. What would "come and see" look like as your own response to someone's doubts?
