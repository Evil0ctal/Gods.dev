Second John runs thirteen verses, John's shortest surviving letter, and he uses that small space efficiently: two commands, one open, one closed. Walk in truth and love one another, he says first. Then, a few verses later, he tells the recipient not to open her door to anyone who arrives without "this teaching." In a book about love, that second command reads almost like a contradiction, until you notice it isn't really about hospitality in general — it's about who gets to teach in your house.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2john 1`

## Whoever "the chosen lady" is

John addresses this to "the chosen lady and her children," language that could name an actual woman and her household, or could be a coded way of addressing a local congregation as a family. Either way, the letter reads intensely personal even at its most guarded.

## Truth as the shape love takes

For John, love was never a feeling detached from content. Walking in truth isn't a separate virtue running alongside love — it's the track love runs on.

> *"This is love, that we should walk according to his commandments."* — 2 John 1:6

## Why the door stays shut

The traveling teachers John warns against aren't strangers with different opinions; they deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, and itinerant teachers in that world depended on host homes to spread their teaching further. Receiving one into your house wasn't neutral hospitality — it was funding the message.

> *"If anyone comes to you, and doesn't bring this teaching, don't receive him into your house, and don't welcome him."* — 2 John 1:10

## Questions for study

1. Why might refusing hospitality to certain teachers actually be an act of love rather than a failure of it?
2. What does it mean that John ties love directly to obedience rather than treating them as separate?
3. Where do you draw the line today between generous hospitality and enabling harm?
