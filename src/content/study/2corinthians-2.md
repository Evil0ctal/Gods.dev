Something had happened in Corinth — a specific offense against Paul or the church, never fully explained — serious enough that Paul had written a painful letter about it (2:3-4) and the church had responded with discipline against the person responsible. By the time we reach chapter 2, the discipline has apparently worked, and Paul's concern shifts entirely: don't let justified correction curdle into permanent exclusion.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2corinthians 2`

## Forgiveness has a deadline

> *"You should rather forgive him and comfort him, lest by any means such a one should be swallowed up with his excessive sorrow."* — 2 Corinthians 2:7

This is a striking reversal of instinct — Paul had pushed hard for accountability, and now pushes just as hard for its completion. Discipline without an eventual return to forgiveness isn't discipline anymore; it's just punishment with no exit, and Paul names the risk explicitly: sorrow can drown a person if it's never allowed to end.

## Satan's oldest trick isn't temptation to sin

> *"That no advantage may be gained over us by Satan; for we are not ignorant of his schemes."* — 2 Corinthians 2:11

Paul locates the danger not in the original offense but in the church's potential response to it — unforgiveness is the scheme here, not indulgence. A community that knows how to discipline but not how to restore has a blind spot exactly where the enemy is aiming.

## One aroma, two effects

> *"To the one a stench from death to death, to the other a sweet aroma from life to life."* — 2 Corinthians 2:16

Paul's image of ministry as a fragrance carried through a Roman triumphal procession is vivid: the same scent that celebrates victors can signal doom for captives. The gospel isn't neutral in its effect — it clarifies, one way or the other, and Paul admits plainly that this is "sufficient for these things" only by grace, not confidence.

## Questions for study

1. Is there a situation where you've disciplined or distanced from someone appropriately, but never completed the return to forgiveness?
2. Where might unforgiveness, rather than temptation, be the "advantage" being gained over you right now?
3. What would it mean to actually "comfort" someone you were previously right to confront?
