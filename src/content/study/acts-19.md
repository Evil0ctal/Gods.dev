Somewhere in Ephesus, seven brothers try to cast out an evil spirit by invoking "Jesus whom Paul preaches" (19:13) — treating the name like a spell rather than an appeal to someone they actually know. The spirit's answer is the most unsettling line in the chapter: *"Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?"* (19:15). The man overpowers all seven, and they run out "naked and wounded." A name without relationship turns out to be no protection at all.

## Two years in a lecture hall

Before that, Paul settles into unglamorous routine: finding disciples who'd only heard of John's baptism, laying hands on them, then "reasoning daily in the school of Tyrannus" for two years (19:9-10), until "all those who lived in Asia heard the word." Extraordinary reach, built from ordinary daily teaching.

## Fear, and burned books

Word of the Sceva incident spreads, and "fear fell on them all" (19:17) — not manipulated fear, but the appropriate reaction to power that isn't a toy. Believers who had practiced magic bring their books and burn them publicly, at a cost of fifty thousand pieces of silver (19:19). Repentance, here, is expensive and visible.

## When the gospel threatens the gift shop

The chapter ends in a riot: a silversmith named Demetrius, sensing his idol-shrine business threatened, whips up two hours of chanting, "Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!" (19:28-34). The gospel's real opposition in Ephesus isn't philosophical — it's economic.

## Questions for study

1. What's the difference between using Jesus's name in faith and using it as the sons of Sceva did?
2. Why might two years of "daily reasoning" have done more than any single dramatic event?
3. What made burning the magic books a necessary, costly act rather than a symbolic gesture?
4. Where might the gospel threaten someone's livelihood or business today, the way it threatened Demetrius's trade?
