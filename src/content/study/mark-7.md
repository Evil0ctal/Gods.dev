The complaint that opens chapter 7 is about unwashed hands, but the argument Jesus has isn't about hygiene — it's about where defilement originates. The Pharisees have built a fence of tradition around the law ("the tradition of the elders," 7:3-4), and Jesus goes straight at the fence rather than the law itself, quoting Isaiah on lips that honor and hearts that don't (7:6).

## Inside out

His verdict reverses the whole framework people had built around clean and unclean:

> *"There is nothing from outside of the man, that going into him can defile him; but the things which proceed out of him are those that defile the man."* — Mark 7:15

Mark adds a parenthetical the disciples clearly needed spelled out: "This he said, making all foods clean" (7:19) — an entire dietary universe reorganized in one sentence, because the contamination Jesus is worried about is evil thoughts, theft, murder, deceit (7:21-22), not what's on the menu.

## A woman who won't take no for an answer

The geography shifts to Gentile territory, and a Syrophoenician woman asks Jesus to heal her daughter. His first answer sounds like a refusal dressed as a proverb — children's bread shouldn't go to dogs (7:27). Her reply doesn't argue the metaphor; it works inside it:

> *"Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs."* — Mark 7:28

She wins the exchange, and her daughter is healed at a distance, without Jesus ever visiting the house.

## Questions for study

1. What "tradition of the elders" — inherited rule rather than actual command — do you still treat as sacred?
2. If defilement comes from within, what's the honest inventory of what's coming out of you lately?
3. The Syrophoenician woman argued her way into the blessing rather than walking away offended. What does her persistence teach about prayer?
4. Read 7:31-37, the healing that ends the chapter. Why do you think Jesus "sighed" (7:34) before he spoke the word?
