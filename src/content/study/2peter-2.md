Peter doesn't waste time describing what these false teachers believe — he describes what they do. Greed, exploitation with "made-up words" (2:3), and lives soaked in indulgence come first; the doctrinal content is almost secondary to the behavior. It's a diagnostic move worth noticing: throughout this chapter, Peter treats conduct as the reliable evidence, more trustworthy than whatever claims a teacher makes about their own authority.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2peter 2`

## Precedent from the beginning

Verses 4-9 run through fallen angels, the flood, and Sodom and Gomorrah — not as random examples but as a pattern Peter wants his readers to see clearly: God has always been capable of judging the unrighteous while rescuing the righteous in the very same act, as with Noah and Lot. The point isn't merely that judgment is coming; it's that rescue and judgment have always traveled together.

## Freedom that enslaves

The chapter's sharpest irony lands in verses 18-19, where these teachers promise freedom while being themselves slaves to corruption — "of whom a man is overcome, by the same he is also brought into bondage." Peter is naming a familiar trap: teaching sold as liberation that actually tightens the very chains it claims to break.

> *"They promise them liberty, while they themselves are bondservants of corruption."* — 2 Peter 2:19

## Questions for study

1. Why does Peter focus so heavily on the behavior of false teachers rather than cataloging their specific doctrinal errors?
2. What does the pairing of judgment and rescue in Noah's and Lot's stories (2:5-9) suggest about how God works even now?
3. Where have you seen teaching that promised freedom but actually produced a new kind of bondage, as verse 19 describes?
