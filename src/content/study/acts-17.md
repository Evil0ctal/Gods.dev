Luke pays the Bereans an unusual compliment: they are "more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so" (17:11). Not gullible, not cynical — they take the claim seriously enough to check it themselves. That combination, eager to hear and willing to verify, is treated here as the ideal response to a new teaching, not a suspicious one.

## A city full of idols

Athens provokes Paul differently — "his spirit was provoked within him as he saw the city full of idols" (17:16). It's not annoyance at being ignored; it's grief at seeing devotion spent on the wrong things. Athenian curiosity, it turns out, is a different obstacle than either Berean sincerity or Thessalonian hostility: they "spent their time in nothing else, but either to tell or to hear some new thing" (17:21) — interested in ideas, not necessarily changed by them.

## Borrowed words, new meaning

At the Areopagus, Paul starts from their own altar "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD" and their own poets: *"For in him we live, move, and have our being... For we are also his offspring"* (17:28). He doesn't import foreign vocabulary; he redirects language they already had toward the God they didn't know they were describing.

## A mixed verdict

The response splits three ways at the mention of resurrection: mockery, polite deferral ("we want to hear you again"), and a few who believe, named individually — Dionysius, Damaris. No mass revival, just real, mixed, human response.

## Questions for study

1. What made the Berean response "noble" rather than merely credulous?
2. How is Athenian curiosity about "new things" different from genuine openness to truth?
3. What do you make of Paul's strategy of quoting the Athenians' own poets back to them?
4. Which of the three responses at the Areopagus — mockery, deferral, belief — is hardest for you to have compassion toward?
