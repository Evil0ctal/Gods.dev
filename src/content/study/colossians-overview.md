Epaphras planted the church at Colossae, not Paul — by the time this letter arrives, Paul has likely never set foot in the town. That distance matters, because what Colossae needed wasn't a personal visit; it needed clarity. Someone there was teaching a layered spirituality — angels, visions, food rules, calendar observances — as additions to Christ. Paul's answer is not a rulebook. It's a hymn.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible colossians 1`

## The hymn does the heavy lifting (1:15-20)

Before Paul argues anything, he sings it: Christ is the image of the invisible God, before all things, the one in whom all things hold together, the head of the church, the firstborn from the dead. Every rival claim in the letter — angels, rulers, "the elemental spirits" — gets answered in advance by a description of Christ too large to need supplementing.

## Complete, not supplemented (chapters 1-2)

The letter's key word is fullness. Not partial access to God through Christ plus something else, but the whole fullness of deity dwelling in him bodily, and believers made full in him. Colossians is written against subtraction dressed up as addition — every extra requirement is really a quiet claim that Christ wasn't enough.

## What fullness looks like on a Tuesday (chapters 3-4)

The back half turns domestic fast: put off old habits, put on new ones, let the peace of Christ rule, and specific instructions for households and workplaces. High Christology, in this letter, is never left floating — it lands on the way a Colossian spoke to their spouse, their slave, their employer.

> *"He is before all things, and in him all things are held together."* — Colossians 1:17

## Questions for study

1. What "extras" — practices, achievements, associations — are you tempted to add to a complete Christ?
2. Read the hymn in 1:15-20 slowly, once, out loud. Which line is hardest to actually believe?
3. Where does the letter's high theology land in the most ordinary part of your week?
4. Epaphras planted a church he then had to write about from a distance. What does that say about how faith gets passed on?
