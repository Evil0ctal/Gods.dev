Paul knows he is going to die, and says so plainly: "the time of my departure has come" (4:6). What's striking about chapter 4 is how it moves — from a solemn charge to preach the word, straight into a shopping list: bring the cloak, bring the books, especially the parchments (4:13). Grand theology and cold weather occupy the same paragraph.

## Preach it whether it's wanted or not

The charge opens with urgency that assumes resistance — "be urgent in season and out of season" (4:2) — because a time is coming when people will trade sound doctrine for teachers who tell them what they want to hear:

> *"For the time will come when they will not listen to the sound doctrine, but having itching ears, will heap up for themselves teachers after their own lusts."* — 2 Timothy 4:3

Paul isn't worried about running out of truth to say. He's worried about running out of people willing to hear it.

## Three verbs, past tense

Verse 7 is one of the most quoted lines Paul ever wrote, and it earns the repetition: "I have fought the good fight. I have finished the course. I have kept the faith." Notice the tense — not a hope, a completed report. He isn't asking whether it was worth it. He already knows.

## Alone, but not abandoned

Demas has left him for the world; only Luke remains (4:10-11). At his first defense, "no one came to help me" (4:16) — and yet the chapter's last claim is that the Lord stood by him (4:17). Paul's confidence at the end isn't in his circle. It's in who stayed when the circle thinned.

## Questions for study

1. What would it take for you to say, honestly, "I have finished the course" about something in your life right now?
2. Verse 13's request for a cloak and books — what does it tell you that Paul's last letter has room for the mundane?
3. Where are "itching ears" (4:3) shaping what you're willing to hear, rather than what's true?
4. Who is your "Luke" — the one who stayed when others left?
