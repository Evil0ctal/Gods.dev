Romans is unusual among Paul's letters because he wrote it to a church he'd never visited, planted by people whose names he doesn't get to claim credit for. That distance may be exactly why it reads less like a pastoral fire-fighting letter and more like a full argument, built brick by brick from the ground up.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible romans 1`

## The case for the gospel

Chapters 1-3 build the necessity of the gospel by first establishing the problem: everyone — Gentile idolater and Jewish law-keeper alike — stands guilty.

> *"...for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God."* — Romans 3:23

Paul doesn't get to grace by skipping the diagnosis; he earns it.

## Righteousness by faith

Chapters 3-5 answer with Paul's central claim: righteousness comes as a gift, received by faith, demonstrated in Abraham long before the law existed to demand anything (chapter 4). Chapter 5 pushes it further — this isn't a private transaction but a cosmic one, one man's trespass and one man's obedience each cascading onto everyone connected to them.

## Union, struggle, and Spirit

Chapters 6-8 turn from courtroom to union — dying and rising with Christ, wrestling honestly with a divided will (chapter 7), and landing in chapter 8's declaration that nothing in creation can separate a believer from God's love.

## Israel, and then real life

Chapters 9-11 wrestle with Israel's place in the story, ending not in a formula but in worship. Chapters 12-15 turn theology into practice — living sacrifice, love as an unpayable debt, bearing with the weak. Chapter 16, mostly names, reminds you the whole argument was addressed to actual people.

## Questions for study

1. Why might a letter to strangers produce Paul's most systematic writing?
2. Which section of the letter's arc — guilt, grace, union, Israel, or practice — do you find yourself returning to most?
3. How does knowing the whole shape of Romans change how you'll read any single chapter of it?
