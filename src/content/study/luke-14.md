Jesus is a dinner guest at a leading Pharisee's house, being watched closely, and heals a man with dropsy on the Sabbath before anyone can even ask whether it's lawful — he asks them first, and they have nothing to say. Then he notices the other guests picking out the seats of honor and tells them to take the lowest seat instead and let the host move them up, rather than risk the public embarrassment of being moved down.

## An invitation list with no return on investment

He pushes the same logic further with the host: don't invite people who can pay you back with a return invitation; invite the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind — people who can't reciprocate at all. The reward, he says, comes at a resurrection you won't be paid back for in this life.

## Excuses at a banquet

A parable follows about a great banquet where the invited guests all beg off with excuses — a field, some oxen, a marriage — and the host fills the seats instead with the poor and the outsiders from the streets and hedges.

## Counting the cost

The chapter ends on discipleship's price tag: a builder who doesn't finish his tower becomes a joke, a king who doesn't count his forces before battle sues for peace too late. Salt that's lost its flavor is fit for nothing.

> *"Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted."* — Luke 14:11

> *"Which of you, desiring to build a tower, doesn't first sit down and count the cost?"* — Luke 14:28

## Questions for study

1. Why does Jesus ask the Pharisees about Sabbath healing before he heals, rather than after?
2. What is the logic behind inviting guests who can't repay you?
3. What do the three excuses in the banquet parable have in common?
4. What would it actually mean to "count the cost" of discipleship in your own life right now?
