Jesus doesn't wander into Jerusalem — he sends disciples ahead for a specific donkey and colt, deliberately fulfilling Zechariah's picture of a king who comes "humble, and riding on a donkey" (21:5). It's the one moment in the Gospels where Jesus stage-manages a public spectacle, and he chooses to stage it as an anti-triumph: no warhorse, no army, just a colt someone else owns.

## Tables and a fig tree, both indicted

The crowd's hosannas barely settle before Jesus is in the temple overturning the tables of money changers: *"It is written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer,' but you have made it a den of robbers"* (21:13). The next morning he curses a fig tree "full of leaves" but fruitless, and it withers on the spot (21:19). Leaves without fruit, worship without honesty — the two acted parables aim at the same target.

## Two sons and a question that answers itself

Asked by what authority he does these things, Jesus answers with a story instead of a credential: two sons told to work in the vineyard, one who says no and goes anyway, one who says yes and doesn't (21:28-31). The religious leaders are cornered by their own answer — the tax collectors and prostitutes who repented, Jesus says, are entering the kingdom ahead of them.

## The tenants who wanted the inheritance

The chapter's harshest parable follows: tenants who beat the landowner's servants and finally kill his son, thinking *"Let's kill him, and seize his inheritance"* (21:38). The chief priests recognize themselves in the story and, rather than repenting, start looking for a way to arrest him.

## Questions for study

1. What would it mean to enter this week the way Jesus entered Jerusalem — without needing the show of power?
2. Where in your own life is there leaf without fruit — activity that looks religious but produces nothing?
3. Which son from 21:28-31 describes you more honestly right now?
4. The tenants wanted the inheritance without the owner. Where are you tempted to want God's gifts without God?
