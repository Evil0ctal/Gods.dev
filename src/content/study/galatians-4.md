Paul opens with a scenario every reader would recognize: a child who will
inherit everything but "is a child, is no different from a bondservant,
though he is lord of all; but is under guardians and stewards until the
day appointed by the father" (4:1-2). The heir and the servant look
identical for a while. What changes everything is not effort or
promotion — it's a date the father already set.

## The fullness of time

*"But when the fullness of the time came, God sent out his Son, born to
a woman, born under the law, that he might redeem those who were under
the law, that we might receive the adoption of children."* — Galatians
4:4-5

The word Paul uses for adoption isn't casual; it names a formal, legal
transfer of status. And immediately after it comes something startlingly
intimate: "God sent out the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying,
'Abba, Father!'" (4:6) — a child's word, not a legal term, arriving right
after the legal transaction is finalized. Status and intimacy, in the
same breath.

## A personal appeal

The chapter turns unexpectedly tender in verses 12-20. Paul recalls
being welcomed by the Galatians "as an angel of God" despite an illness
that first brought him to them, and now feels like he's "again in
travail" over their drift (4:19). It's the warmest, most vulnerable
paragraph in the letter.

## Two women, two covenants

Paul closes with the allegory of Hagar and Sarah — slave woman and free
woman, standing in for two different ways of relating to God: one born
"according to the flesh," one "through promise" (4:22-23). The point
isn't ethnicity; it's whether your standing rests on your own effort or
on a promise made to you.

## Questions for study

1. Where in your life do you still function like a servant when your
   actual status has already changed?
2. What does it mean that legal adoption and the cry "Abba, Father"
   arrive in the very same sentence?
3. Which "covenant" — effort or promise — actually governs how you
   relate to God day to day?
