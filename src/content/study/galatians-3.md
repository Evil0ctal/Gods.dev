Paul opens with a genuine insult: "Foolish Galatians, who has bewitched
you, that you should not obey the truth?" (3:1). "Bewitched" is the
word — not merely mistaken, but under a spell, talked out of something
they'd clearly seen with their own eyes: "Jesus Christ was openly set
forth crucified among you." Whatever argument had reached them, Paul
treats it less as a theological error and more as a kind of hypnosis.

## The experiment already ran

His first move is almost clinical: did you receive the Spirit "by the
works of the law, or by hearing of faith?" (3:2). He's pointing at their
own experience as evidence — the Spirit came before the law-keeping
argument ever showed up. If the experiment already produced a result,
why redesign the process now?

## Abraham, before the law existed

The chapter's real engine is Abraham, cited centuries before Moses ever
received the law: "Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for
righteousness" (3:6). Paul's argument is chronological as much as
theological — righteousness by faith isn't a New Testament innovation;
it's the original arrangement, and the law that came 430 years later
"does not annul a covenant" already ratified (3:17).

## A guardian, not the point

Paul calls the law a *paidagogos* — a household guardian who escorts a
child to school but isn't the teacher (3:24-25). Its job was custodial
and temporary, "until faith would be revealed." Once that arrival
happens, the guardian's job is done.

*"There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man,
there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ
Jesus."* — Galatians 3:28

## Questions for study

1. What "bewitching" arguments have talked you out of something you'd
   already seen clearly?
2. Paul appeals to lived experience as evidence before he appeals to
   Scripture. When is that a fair move, and when is it not?
3. Where do inherited categories — status, ethnicity, gender — still
   quietly rank people in your own circles, despite 3:28?
