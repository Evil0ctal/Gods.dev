The charge against Peter when he returns to Jerusalem isn't heresy — it's dinner. "You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them!" (11:3). A meal has become a doctrinal crisis, which tells you how much theology can hide inside ordinary social boundaries. Peter's defense is simply to narrate what happened, step by step, and let the sequence of events make the argument for him.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible acts 11`

## An argument from what already happened

Peter doesn't cite a new principle; he retells the vision, then the Spirit falling on Cornelius's household exactly as it had on the apostles at Pentecost. His conclusion is almost a shrug: *"If then God gave to them the same gift as us when we believed in the Lord Jesus Christ, who was I, that I could withstand God?"* (11:17). The critics have nowhere left to stand and they know it — they fall silent and glorify God.

## A name outsiders gave them

Meanwhile in Antioch, without any committee deciding it, "the disciples were first called Christians" (11:26) — likely a label from outsiders, maybe mocking, that the church simply absorbed and kept. Identity, sometimes, is given to you before you choose it.

## Relief before doctrine settles

Before the bigger argument over Gentile inclusion is even formally resolved (that's next chapter's business, in ch. 15), a famine prophecy moves this same fledgling, ethnically mixed church to send money to believers in Judea (11:29-30). Generosity outran the paperwork.

## Questions for study

1. Why might a shared meal carry more theological weight than a shared doctrine?
2. Peter's argument was simply "this already happened." When is testimony a better argument than theory?
3. What does it mean that "Christian" was likely a name given by outsiders, not chosen by the church itself?
4. How does the famine relief in 11:27-30 model unity ahead of full agreement?
