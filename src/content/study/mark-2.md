The house is so packed that four men carrying a paralyzed friend can't get near the door, so they climb onto the roof and dig through it, lowering the man on his mat directly in front of Jesus (2:3-4). It's an absurd, destructive, completely committed piece of persistence — someone's roof is now a hole — and Mark records it approvingly, with no comment on the property damage. What Jesus notices first isn't the paralysis; it's the persistence: *"seeing their faith"* (2:5), he forgives the man's sins before he heals his legs.

## A diagnosis before the cure

The scribes object internally — only God can forgive sins — and Jesus answers the unspoken thought directly, then heals the man to prove the authority behind the forgiveness (2:8-11). The healing is almost the easier miracle to accept; the forgiveness is the claim that actually offends people.

## Dinner with the wrong crowd

Jesus calls Levi, a tax collector, and then eats at his house with "many tax collectors and sinners" (2:15) — professionally despised company. Challenged on it, Jesus answers plainly: *"Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance"* (2:17). It reframes the whole complaint: of course he's there. That's the job.

## A day built for people, not the reverse

The chapter closes with grain plucked on the Sabbath and the objection that follows, answered with one of Jesus' most quietly radical lines: *"The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath"* (2:27) — a rule reordered around the people it was meant to serve.

## Questions for study

1. What would "digging through a roof" look like for you, on behalf of someone who can't get to help on their own?
2. Why might forgiveness be the more offensive claim in this story, harder to accept than the physical healing?
3. Who's the "wrong crowd" you'd feel exposed being seen with, the way the Pharisees judged Jesus at Levi's table?
4. Where in your own life has a good rule become more important than the person it was meant to serve?
