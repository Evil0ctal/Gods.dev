Paul made tents for a living, and it shows here. He calls the body an
"earthly house of our tent" (5:1) — not a permanent structure, something
you pitch and strike, weatherworn and temporary. That is an odd way to
describe your own body, and it is the hinge of the chapter: what looks
like loss from inside the tent looks different from outside it.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2corinthians 5`

## Groaning, not despairing

Verses 1-5 admit something churches often rush past: mortality is
genuinely burdensome. "We groan, being burdened" (5:4) is not weak
faith — it is honest faith. Paul doesn't argue the tent never leaks. He
argues a building is coming, "not made with hands, eternal, in the
heavens" (5:1), and the groaning is the ache of a guarantee already
given, the Spirit as down payment (5:5).

## The math changes

*"If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation. The old things have
passed away. Behold, all things have become new."* — 2 Corinthians 5:17

Verse 17 gets quoted alone often enough that its neighbors get forgotten.
It sits inside an argument about reconciliation, not self-improvement:
God was in Christ "not reckoning to them their trespasses" (5:19), and
now hands that same ledger-correcting message to ordinary people, calling
them ambassadors (5:20). An ambassador doesn't invent the message he
carries; he delivers someone else's terms into a room he didn't build.

## The exchange at the center

Verse 21 is the theological center of gravity for the whole letter: the
sinless one "made to be sin" so that sinners might "become the
righteousness of God." Six words, stated once, that most of theology
spends volumes unpacking.

## Questions for study

1. Where in your life does "groaning" coexist with genuine hope, rather
   than cancel it out?
2. What changes if you think of yourself as carrying someone else's
   message rather than promoting your own?
3. Sit slowly with the exchange in 5:21. What would it cost to take it
   literally this week?
