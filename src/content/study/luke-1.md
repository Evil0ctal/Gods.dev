Luke opens like no other Gospel: not a genealogy, not a poem, but a historian's preface — "having traced the course of all things accurately from the first," writing so that Theophilus "may know the certainty" of what he'd been taught (1:3-4). Before a single miracle happens, Luke tells you he's done his homework. Then, having established his method, he immediately writes about an angel, a barren woman, and a virgin — and never once treats the two modes as in tension.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 1`

## An old priest struck silent

Zechariah, a priest well past the age of surprises, is told his wife Elizabeth will bear a son, and he asks for proof (1:18). He gets it, but not the kind he wanted — struck mute until the birth, a nine-month silence that turns a skeptical question into an enforced season of waiting.

## A young woman's yes

Mary's response to a far stranger announcement is the opposite of Zechariah's: no demand for proof, just a question about mechanics ("how can this be?", 1:34), then acceptance.

> *"Mary said, 'Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word.'"* — Luke 1:38

## A song about reversal

Once Mary reaches Elizabeth, she breaks into song, and the Magnificat already sounds the note Luke will keep returning to across his whole Gospel — God siding with the low and humbling the proud:

> *"He has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly."* — Luke 1:52

## Questions for study

1. Luke insists on careful, orderly investigation before he tells you anything miraculous. Does that change how you read what follows?
2. Zechariah asks for proof and receives silence instead. What might his nine months of quiet have taught him?
3. Mary's yes comes without demanding evidence first. What would it cost you to answer that way?
4. The Magnificat already previews Luke's whole theme of reversal. Where do you long to see the proud humbled and the lowly lifted?
