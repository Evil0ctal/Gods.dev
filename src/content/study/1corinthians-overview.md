1 Corinthians isn't a theological treatise Paul decided to write — it's a series of responses to a specific, troubled congregation, and you can feel the difference on every page. Corinth was wealthy, cosmopolitan, and spiritually gifted (1:5-7), and also split into celebrity factions, tolerating a sexual scandal it should have grieved over, suing its own members in court, and ranking each other by whose gifts looked more impressive. Paul doesn't write around any of this. He writes straight into it.

## The argument holding it together

> *"I beg you, brothers... that you all speak the same thing, and that there be no divisions among you."* — 1 Corinthians 1:10

Division is the wound Paul keeps returning to, in forms that shift chapter to chapter: division by favorite teacher (1-4), division by lawsuit and sexual ethics (5-6), division by marital status and diet (7-10), division by worship practice and spiritual gift (11-14). The cross itself becomes his answer in chapter 1 — "the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are dying, but to us who are saved it is the power of God" (1:18) — a foundation too humbling for any faction to claim as its own trophy.

## A structure built from real questions

Much of the letter follows a "now concerning" pattern (7:1, 8:1, 12:1) — Paul is visibly working through a list, whether from a letter the Corinthians sent or reports he'd received. That gives the book its patchwork shape but also its usefulness: almost no modern church question about ethics, worship, or community life goes untouched somewhere in these sixteen chapters.

## The center and the horizon

Chapter 13's meditation on love sits at the argument's structural center, between two chapters on spiritual gifts, redefining what actually matters. Chapter 15's resurrection defense sits near the end, giving the whole letter its horizon: none of the practical corrections matter if the resurrection isn't real, and everything matters more because it is.

## Questions for study

1. Which faction or division from chapter 1-4 has a modern equivalent in your own experience of church?
2. Read the "now concerning" sections (7:1, 8:1, 12:1) — what does Paul's method of answering questions teach about handling disagreement?
3. Why might Paul have placed the love chapter between two chapters about spiritual gifts rather than somewhere else in the letter?
