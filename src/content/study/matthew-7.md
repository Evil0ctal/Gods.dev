Matthew 7 opens with the most quoted, least contextualized verse in the sermon — "judge not, that you not be judged" — and immediately complicates it. Jesus isn't banning discernment; two verses later he's telling people to remove the log from their own eye specifically so they can see clearly enough to help with someone else's speck (7:3-5). The prohibition is against a certain kind of judgment: the hypocritical, self-exempting kind, not evaluation itself.

## Ask, seek, knock — and the reversal that follows

Jesus grounds prayer in the character of the Father, using an argument from the lesser to the greater: if flawed human parents know how to give good gifts, "how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him" (7:11). Right after this comes the Golden Rule, and Matthew places it here on purpose — asking God for good things and treating others well are not separate topics.

> *"All things therefore whatever you desire for men to do to you, you shall also do to them; for this is the law and the prophets."* — Matthew 7:12

## Fruit, foundations, and no third option

The chapter closes with three pairs: two gates (narrow and wide), two trees (known by their fruit, not their branding), and two builders (rock and sand). All three images share a structure — outward appearance is not the test; outcome is. The wise and foolish builders in the final parable both hear the same words; the storm hits both houses equally. What differs is what was actually done with what was heard.

## Questions for study

1. Where do you tend to confuse "don't judge" with "don't discern," and where do those two get properly separated?
2. Jesus argues from human parenting to divine generosity. Does that argument change how you pray?
3. The wise and foolish builders both heard the sermon. What is the gap in your own life between hearing these words and doing them?
