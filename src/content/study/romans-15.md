Chapter 14's argument about disputable matters doesn't end at the chapter break — Romans 15 opens still inside it: "we who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak, and not to please ourselves" (15:1). Paul roots this in Christ's own pattern — "even Christ didn't please himself" (15:3) — which turns an argument about diet into an argument about the shape of love itself.

## One mouth, one voice

Paul's prayer for unity is specific about its purpose: "that with one accord you may with one mouth glorify the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (15:6). Unity, in his telling, isn't the goal — it's the byproduct of a shared, louder purpose. Churches don't get along by focusing on getting along; they get along by focusing together on something bigger than their disagreements.

## The traveler behind the letter

Then Paul does something unusual for an ancient letter: he shows his itinerary. He's finished his work in the east, he's carrying a collection to Jerusalem for the poor there, and after that he wants to go west — all the way to Spain, with Rome as a planned stop along the way (15:23-28). This is a working missionary, not a distant theologian, mapping out his next several years in real time.

## Priestly service

> *"...that I should be a servant of Christ Jesus to the Gentiles, serving as a priest of the Good News of God, that the offering up of the Gentiles might be made acceptable, sanctified by the Holy Spirit."* — Romans 15:16

## Questions for study

1. Whose "weakness" are you currently being asked to bear rather than dismiss?
2. What would it look like for your community to pursue "one voice" by focusing on something bigger than its disagreements?
3. Paul plans concretely, in public, and asks for prayer over the plan (15:30-32). What's your version of that kind of transparent, prayed-over planning?
