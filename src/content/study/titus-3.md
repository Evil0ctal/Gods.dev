Chapter 3 opens with a mundane civic instruction — be subject to rulers, obedient, ready for good work (3:1) — before Paul does something he does often in his letters: he reminds his readers what they used to be. "We were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived... hateful, and hating one another" (3:3). The "we" matters. He isn't lecturing from above; he's remembering from inside.

## Not earned, washed

Verses 4-7 pivot hard against that memory:

> *"Not by works of righteousness which we did ourselves, but according to his mercy, he saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewing by the Holy Spirit."* — Titus 3:5

"Washing" is a physical, almost domestic image for something Paul refuses to let anyone claim credit for. The whole passage works like a hinge: here's who we were, here's what happened to us, and neither half was our doing.

## Arguments not worth the room

The chapter's practical close is almost anticlimactic after that: avoid "foolish questionings, genealogies, strife, and disputes about the law," because they are "unprofitable and vain" (3:9). After the height of verses 4-7, Paul brings Titus back down to something ordinary and specific — don't let your church burn its energy on unwinnable arguments.

## A factious man gets a limit, not a purge

Verse 10 gives a process, not a purge: two warnings, then avoidance. Even correction here has a shape and a stopping point rather than an open-ended campaign.

## Questions for study

1. Why might Paul lead with "we were also once foolish" (3:3) before describing the change?
2. What does "washing of regeneration" (3:5) protect against, if you tried to earn it instead?
3. What "foolish questionings" (3:9) drain energy in your own community that could go toward "good works" (3:8) instead?
4. Two warnings, then step back (3:10) — where in your life could that pattern replace an open-ended conflict?
