Jesus told this story to an audience that was already annoyed with him.
Luke sets the scene in one line: *"Now all the tax collectors and sinners
were coming close to him to hear him. The Pharisees and the scribes
murmured"* (Luke 15:1-2). The parable of the prodigal son is not a warm
bedtime story. It is a pointed answer to a complaint: **why does God
welcome the wrong people?**

> Read it first, in this very terminal: `bible luke 15:11-32`

## The insult

The younger son's request — *"Father, give me my share of your property"*
(15:12) — is not just rude. In the world of the text, asking for your
inheritance while your father is alive is functionally saying: *I wish you
were dead. Your money means more to me than you do.*

The father's response is the first shock of the story. No lecture, no
disowning, no negotiation. *"He divided his livelihood between them."* He
absorbs the insult and lets the boy go. Whatever else this story teaches,
it starts here: love that refuses to imprison.

## The far country

The son burns through everything in "riotous living," and then a famine
does what famines do. He ends up feeding pigs — for a Jewish audience, the
bottom of the barrel, ritual uncleanness included — and envying the pigs'
menu (15:16).

Notice what brings him home. It is not repentance in its purest form; it
is hunger. His rehearsed speech (*"make me as one of your hired
servants"*, 15:19) is half contrition, half survival strategy. The story
is honest about this, and that honesty is good news: you do not need
perfect motives to head home. You just need to head home.

## The father who runs

Here is the verse the whole parable pivots on:

> *"But while he was still far off, his father saw him, and was moved with
> compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him."* — Luke 15:20

A dignified landowner in that culture did not run. Running meant hitching
up your robe, showing your legs, becoming a spectacle. The son had
prepared a speech; the father interrupts it with a robe, a ring, and a
feast. The son asks to be a servant; the father restores him as a son
before he can finish the sentence.

If you want a one-line summary of the New Testament's picture of God,
this is a strong candidate: **the father sees you while you are still far
off, which means he was watching the road.**

## The other lost son

The story does not end with the party. The elder brother — obedient,
hardworking, furious — refuses to come in. His complaint (15:29-30) is
the Pharisees' complaint from verse 2, dramatized: *I did everything
right, and you threw a feast for the one who did everything wrong.*

The father's answer is tender: *"Son, you are always with me, and all
that is mine is yours."* The parable is left deliberately unfinished — we
never learn whether the elder brother goes inside. That is because the
ending belongs to the listener. Both sons were lost: one in a far
country, one in the front yard. Grace offends the second kind more.

## Questions for study

1. Which brother do you instinctively sympathize with — and what does
   that tell you?
2. The son's motives for returning were mixed at best. Does that change
   how you read the father's welcome? Should it?
3. What would it look like, concretely, to "watch the road" for someone
   this week?
4. Read Luke 15 as a whole (`bible luke 15`) — three lost things, three
   findings, three parties. What escalates across the three stories?
