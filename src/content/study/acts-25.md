Festus, brand new to the job, wants to do his Jewish constituents a
favor and asks Paul if he'd be willing to stand trial in Jerusalem
instead of Caesarea (25:9). It's a small, political request, and Paul
recognizes instantly what it actually means — a return trip through
territory where forty men had already sworn an oath to kill him. His
answer is four words that reroute the rest of the book: "I appeal to
Caesar" (25:11).

> Read it in the terminal: `bible acts 25`

## A citizen using his citizenship

Paul has never been shy about invoking his Roman rights when it serves
the gospel's advance rather than his comfort — chapter 22 showed him do
it to avoid an illegal flogging. Here he does it again, and Festus, oddly
relieved, agrees: "You have appealed to Caesar. You shall go to Caesar"
(25:12). One sentence ends the legal maneuvering of three chapters and
guarantees, by Roman law, that Paul is going to Rome.

## A hearing with no charge that sticks

Festus later admits to King Agrippa something almost comic in its
honesty: he has to send a prisoner to Caesar but has no charges "worth
writing" (25:26-27). The whole apparatus of accusation — the Sanhedrin,
Tertullus, two years of custody — collapses under a simple procedural
requirement. You need an actual crime to write in the paperwork, and
there isn't one.

> *"For it seems to me unreasonable, in sending a prisoner, not to also
> specify the charges against him."* — Acts 25:27

## Agrippa's curiosity

King Agrippa's line — "I also would like to hear the man myself"
(25:22) — sets up next chapter's speech, but notice what's happening
structurally. Every person in this chain of custody, hostile or
indifferent, ends up wanting to hear Paul speak. The gospel keeps
generating its own audience, even through people with no interest in
believing it.

## Questions for study

1. Paul uses legal and civic systems strategically without compromising
   his message. Where's the line between wisdom and expediency?
2. Festus can't find an actual charge, yet the process has consumed years
   of Paul's life. What does that tell you about the difference between
   legality and justice?
3. Why do you think even hostile or indifferent figures in Acts keep
   wanting to hear Paul out?
4. Is there a "Caesar" you need to appeal to — a larger, slower process
   you should trust rather than fight in the smaller arena?
