Paul spends two verses on a vision of paradise — caught up to the "third
heaven," hearing "unspeakable words which it is not lawful for a man to
utter" (12:2-4) — and then spends the rest of the chapter on something
far less impressive: a problem he asked God to remove three times and
didn't get to. The mystical experience gets a paragraph. The unanswered
prayer gets the chapter.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2corinthians 12`

## A thorn, unnamed on purpose

Paul never says what the "thorn in the flesh" actually was (12:7), and
that's arguably deliberate — a chronic, specific, physical or
circumstantial affliction that stays vague enough to fit whatever thorn
the reader is currently carrying. What matters more than its identity is
its function: "lest I should be exalted above measure... a messenger of
Satan to buffet me." The vision could have made him insufferable. The
thorn kept him honest.

## The answer that wasn't a yes

*"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in
weakness."* — 2 Corinthians 12:9

Three requests, one answer, and it isn't removal — it's redefinition.
Paul doesn't report feeling better about the thorn; he reports a change
in where he locates strength. "Most gladly therefore will I rather glory
in my weaknesses, that the power of Christ may rest on me" (12:9) is not
resignation. It's a genuine trade: the thorn stays, but so does something
that turns out to matter more.

## Fear before the visit

The chapter's final section (12:19-21) shows Paul's real anxiety ahead
of his next visit — not pride, but fear of finding the church still
tangled in "strife, jealousy, outbursts of anger." Even after the
vision, the mundane sins are what worry him most.

## Questions for study

1. What's your version of a thorn you've asked to have removed three
   times?
2. Does "power made perfect in weakness" sound like comfort or like a
   hard trade to you? Why?
3. Paul worries more about ordinary discord than spectacular failure.
   Where does that ordering apply to your own life?
