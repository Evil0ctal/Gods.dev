The qualifications for a high priest sound almost bureaucratic at first — taken from among men, appointed on their behalf, offering gifts and sacrifices for sins. But then comes the detail worth sitting with: he "can deal gently with the ignorant and wayward, since he himself also is beset with weakness." Shared weakness isn't a disqualifying flaw here. It's built into the job.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible hebrews 5`

## Appointed, not self-appointed

Jesus didn't glorify himself into the role; he was appointed, and the writer pairs two Psalms to make the point — begotten as Son, and made priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. The office came from outside him, even though he was the one uniquely suited to hold it.

## Obedience, learned

> *"Though he was a son, yet learned obedience by the things which he suffered."* — Hebrews 5:8

That's a strange verb to apply to the Son of God — *learned* — but it's exactly what qualifies him to sympathize rather than merely to rule. Loud cries and tears aren't incidental details; they're the price of the sympathy that comes later.

## A rebuke aimed at stalled growth

The tone shifts abruptly: "though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you again the rudiments." People who should be handling solid food are still on milk, unskilled in the word of righteousness — a diagnosis less about intelligence than about neglected practice.

## Questions for study

1. Why might shared weakness make someone a better mediator rather than a worse one?
2. What does it mean that Jesus "learned" obedience rather than simply possessing it?
3. Where in your own faith have you stalled on milk when you should be handling solid food?
