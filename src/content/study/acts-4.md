The council's problem in Acts 4 is not theological, it's evidentiary. They can arrest Peter and John, they can threaten them, but they cannot make the healed man sit back down. Verse 14 says it plainly: seeing him standing there with the apostles, "they could say nothing against it." So the strategy shifts from disproving the miracle to gagging the report of it — which tells you something about how threadbare the opposition actually was.

## Unlearned men, learned answer

Peter and John are described as "unlearned and ignorant" (4:13) — no rabbinic training, no credentials. What the council notices instead is that they had been with Jesus. That's the qualification Luke keeps holding up: not pedigree, but proximity to Christ and fullness of the Spirit.

## The name with no substitute

Peter's answer to "by what power, or in what name" is not diplomatic:

> *"There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given among men, by which we must be saved!"* — Acts 4:12

It's an exclusive claim delivered to the exact authorities who would find it most offensive, and Peter does not soften it to survive the room.

## A prayer, not a strategy

Released and threatened, the believers don't lawyer up — they pray, quoting Psalm 2 back to God and asking not for safety but for boldness. The place shakes; they're filled again. Then comes the detail easy to skip past: people selling land so "there was not a needy person among them" (4:34). Boldness and generosity show up in the same paragraph, as if they're the same reflex.

## Questions for study

1. What does it mean that the apostles' credential was simply "having been with Jesus"?
2. Acts 4:12 is a hard exclusivity claim. How do you hold that alongside genuine respect for people of other faiths?
3. The believers prayed for boldness, not safety. Which would you have prayed for?
4. Why might radical generosity (4:32-35) follow naturally from a Spirit-filled prayer meeting?
