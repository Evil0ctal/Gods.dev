Paul spends chapter 6 insisting sin has lost its grip, then chapter 7 sounds like a man still very much in the fight. He doesn't soften the contradiction — he puts it on the page: he doesn't understand his own actions, doesn't do what he wants, and does the very thing he hates. That's either shockingly honest or shockingly familiar, depending on how closely you've read your own week.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible romans 7`

## The law is not the villain

Paul's first move is a defense of the law itself. Sin used the commandment as a foothold, "finding occasion through the commandment" to deceive and kill (7:11). The law didn't create the sickness; it took a reading and told the truth about it. A speed-limit sign doesn't make you a fast driver — it just measures one.

## The divided man

Then comes the passage that has comforted more people than it should, because misery loves company: two laws at war in one body, willing the good and doing the opposite (7:21-23). Paul isn't describing a phase he outgrows; he's describing what it feels like to take goodness seriously. The people who feel this tension least are usually not trying hardest.

## The only exit

> *"What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death?"* — Romans 7:24

The question isn't rhetorical filler before chapter 8 — it's the whole chapter's argument compressed into one line: self-improvement cannot fix this. Chapter 8's answer starts here, at the confession that the war can't be won from the inside.

## Questions for study

1. Where in your life does Paul's description of doing what he hates land most accurately right now?
2. Does defending the law's goodness while confessing personal failure feel contradictory to you, or does Paul hold both convincingly?
3. What would change if you stopped treating inner conflict as proof you're failing, and started treating it as proof you're paying attention?
