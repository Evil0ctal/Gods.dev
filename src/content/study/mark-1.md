Matthew and Luke open with genealogies and nativity scenes; Mark opens with a man in camel's hair eating locusts in the wilderness. No manger, no shepherds — just John the Baptist, mid-shout, and then Jesus walking straight into the frame to be baptized. Mark's first sentence tells you what kind of book this is: *"The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God"* (1:1), and then it moves like there's no time to waste explaining anything further.

## A day in fast-forward

Watch the word "immediately" — Mark uses it more than any other Gospel, and it's doing real work here. Jesus is baptized, immediately driven into the wilderness, immediately calls fishermen who immediately leave their nets, immediately teaches in a synagogue, immediately casts out a demon. Forty chapters of material in other Gospels get compressed into paragraphs. This isn't sloppy editing — it's a portrait of urgency, a Messiah who doesn't linger.

## Fame he keeps trying to outrun

By the end of chapter 1, Jesus has healed Peter's mother-in-law, cast out demons, cleansed a leper, and the crowds are pressing in so hard "he could no more openly enter into a city" (1:45). Yet in the middle of all that demand, Mark notes something easy to skip past: *"In the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there"* (1:35). The busiest chapter in the book still makes room for solitude before the sun's up.

## Questions for study

1. What does Mark's lack of a birth narrative tell you about what he thinks the "good news" actually is?
2. Pick one "immediately" in this chapter — what would it cost you to respond to a call that fast?
3. Jesus prays before the demands of the day start, not after they're finished. What does your own rhythm usually look like?
4. The leper is told to tell no one but instead spreads the news everywhere (1:44-45). Why might Jesus have wanted quiet, and what does the leper's disobedience cost him?
