Six days after Peter names Jesus "the Christ," three disciples watch his clothes turn "intensely white, like snow" (9:3) on a mountain, and Moses and Elijah appear talking with him. It's the one moment in the Gospels where the curtain over Jesus's ordinary appearance is pulled fully back, and Peter's response is to suggest building something — three shelters, as if the moment could be housed and preserved (9:5). Mark notes drily that Peter said this "for he didn't know what to say" (9:6).

## The voice that redirects

A cloud interrupts the suggestion:

> *"A voice came out of the cloud, 'This is my beloved Son. Listen to him.'"* — Mark 9:7

The last two words do the real work — not "look at him," which the mountain has already accomplished, but *listen to him*, pointing straight back down the mountain toward everything Jesus has said about suffering that Peter doesn't want to hear.

## A father's honest prayer

Down in that valley, a boy with a violent spirit and a father at the end of his rope produce the chapter's most quoted line — not confidence, but its opposite:

> *"Immediately the father of the child cried out with tears, 'I believe. Help my unbelief!'"* — Mark 9:24

It's a strange kind of faith that admits its own shortfall out loud, and Jesus heals the boy anyway.

## Arguing about greatness on the way to a cross

The chapter's last movement is almost comic in its timing: the disciples argue about who is greatest (9:34) right after Jesus has, for the second time, said he's going to be killed. His answer puts a child in their midst — the least strategically useful person in that culture — as the model of greatness (9:36-37).

## Questions for study

1. Peter wants to build shelters and preserve the mountaintop moment. What "shelter" do you try to build around your best spiritual experiences?
2. "I believe. Help my unbelief" holds both statements at once. Does your own faith allow for that honesty?
3. Why do you think the disciples' argument about greatness immediately follows a passion prediction?
4. Jesus puts a child at the center of the definition of greatness. What would that look like applied to your own ambitions?
