Acts 6 opens with an unglamorous problem: Greek-speaking widows are being overlooked in the daily food distribution while Hebrew-speaking widows are not. This is not persecution from outside; it's neglect from inside, and it runs along an ethnic fault line the church would rather not admit exists. What's notable is that nobody minimizes it. The Twelve treat a logistics complaint as serious enough to reorganize the whole community around.

## Two jobs, not one job done badly

The apostles' solution isn't to try harder at everything. It's to separate the work: "we will continue steadfastly in prayer and in the ministry of the word" (6:4), while seven chosen men — "full of the Holy Spirit and of wisdom" — take responsibility for the tables. Spiritual authority and practical competence aren't ranked; they're distributed to the people suited for each.

## Stephen's qualifications

Stephen, the standout of the seven, is introduced as "full of faith and of the Holy Spirit" (6:5) before he's ever recorded doing anything. Serving food turns out not to be a demotion from ministry — for Stephen it's the start of it; he moves straight into "great wonders and signs among the people" (6:8).

## A face that unsettles a council

When false witnesses drag Stephen before the Sanhedrin, the room's reaction is strange: "all who sat in the council... saw his face like it was the face of an angel" (6:15). They came to accuse a criminal and found themselves looking at something they couldn't categorize.

## Questions for study

1. Why might it matter that the apostles fixed the widows' problem structurally instead of just apologizing?
2. What does choosing men "full of the Spirit" for a food-service role say about how the church saw menial work?
3. What do you make of a council staring at "the face of an angel" and still moving to kill him?
4. Where in your own community do practical neglect and spiritual health quietly depend on each other?
