The dispute in Romans 14 sounds almost quaint now — some believers eat anything, others eat only vegetables, some regard certain days as special and others treat every day alike (14:2, 5). Paul doesn't rule on who's technically correct. He rules on how they're supposed to treat each other while disagreeing, which turns out to be the harder and more durable question.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible romans 14`

## Not your servant to judge

"Who are you who judge another's servant? To his own lord he stands or falls" (14:4). Paul's logic is almost administrative: if someone belongs to a different household, you don't get to fire them. The strong believer who eats anything and the weak believer who eats carefully are both, Paul insists, serving the same Lord — just differently.

## Whatever is not of faith

The chapter's sharpest line reframes the whole issue around conscience rather than correctness: "whatever is not of faith is sin" (14:23). That's a demanding standard in both directions — it means acting against your own conviction is sin even if the act itself is neutral, and it means you can't borrow someone else's freedom if your own conscience hasn't caught up.

## The kingdom is bigger than the menu

> *"For God's Kingdom is not eating and drinking, but righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit."* — Romans 14:17

Paul's closing appeal is to give up your right rather than wreck someone else's faith over it (14:15, 21) — a strange, costly kind of love that spends freedom on other people instead of itself.

## Questions for study

1. What's the modern equivalent of the meat-and-days dispute in your own church or circle?
2. Where might you be "the strong" who needs to hold back a freedom for someone else's sake?
3. How do you personally test whether an action is "of faith" for you, per verse 23?
