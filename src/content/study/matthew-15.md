The Pharisees open this chapter with a complaint about hand-washing — a human tradition, not a written command — and Jesus responds by widening the target considerably. He accuses them of using tradition to void an actual commandment (honoring father and mother, sidestepped by a technicality called "Corban") and then relocates the whole conversation about purity: "It is not that which enters into the mouth that defiles the man; but that which proceeds out of the mouth" (15:11). Uncleanness, in his account, comes from the heart outward, not from the outside in.

## Lip service and heart distance

Jesus quotes Isaiah against them directly, and the quote lands like an X-ray:

> *"This people honors me with their lips; but their heart is far from me."* — Matthew 15:8

Correct external practice and actual nearness to God are not the same thing, and the chapter insists on separating them where the Pharisees had let them blur together.

## An outsider who won't be dismissed

Then Jesus travels into Gentile territory, and a Canaanite woman — an ethnic and religious outsider by every category the culture used — begs him to heal her demon-possessed daughter. Jesus initially answers her with silence, then with a statement that sounds like a flat refusal about his mission being "to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (15:24). She doesn't leave. She reframes his own metaphor back at him — even dogs eat the crumbs that fall from the table — and Jesus responds not with irritation but with open admiration:

> *"Woman, great is your faith! Be it done to you even as you desire."* — Matthew 15:28

## Questions for study

1. Where in your own life is external correctness substituting for actual nearness to God?
2. The Canaanite woman kept pressing after what looked like a refusal. What does her persistence teach about the difference between rejection and testing?
3. Jesus calls her faith "great" — greater than anyone else's recorded response so far in Matthew. What made it so?
