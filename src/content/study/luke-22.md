Jesus tells his disciples he has "earnestly desired" to eat this Passover with them before he suffers — an odd thing to say about a meal that both of them know ends with betrayal already in motion. Judas has already made his deal with the chief priests before the bread is even broken. Luke lets that timeline sit uncomfortably close together: the most intimate meal of Jesus's ministry, happening inside a plot already underway.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 22`

## A meal turned into a memorial
Jesus takes ordinary Passover elements — bread, wine — and reassigns their meaning permanently.

> *"This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me."* — Luke 22:19

## An argument about rank, at this table, tonight
Almost immediately after, the disciples start arguing about who is the greatest — at the same table, hours before the arrest. Jesus doesn't scold them for ambition exactly; he redefines what greatness means among them: the one who serves, not the one who reclines and is served.

## Sweat, and a decision
In Gethsemane, Jesus asks for a way out and then chooses the way in anyway. The prayer isn't resignation — it's a real request, refused by himself, in real time.

> *"Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done."* — Luke 22:42

## Questions for study
1. Why might Luke place the disciples' argument about greatness right after the Last Supper?
2. What does Jesus's prayer in Gethsemane teach about the difference between wanting and choosing?
3. How does Peter's coming denial, predicted in this same chapter, complicate his earlier confidence?
4. Where do you need to pray "not my will" honestly, rather than in resigned silence?
