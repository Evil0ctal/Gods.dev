The merchants' lament in this chapter reads like a shipping manifest — gold, silver, jewels, fine linen, cinnamon, wine, cattle, chariots — an inventory of everything Babylon bought and sold (18:12-13). Then the list ends on its final line item: "slaves, and human lives." The whole catalog of luxury goods was always resting on that last entry. The chapter wants you to notice it's there, at the bottom, itemized like everything else.

## Get out before the collapse

> *"Come out of her, my people, that you have no participation in her sins, and that you don't receive of her plagues."* — Revelation 18:4

The call isn't to fight Babylon or fix her. It's simply to leave — separation is the only response offered, issued before the fall, not after.

## Three groups, one lament

Kings, merchants, and sailors each mourn Babylon in turn, and each group's grief is identical in shape: they stand far off, afraid of her torment, crying "woe, woe" over what they've lost (18:9-19). None of them mourn her; they mourn what her fall costs them.

## A stone that doesn't float

An angel throws a stone like a great millstone into the sea: "thus with violence will Babylon the great city be thrown down, and will be found no more at all" (18:21). No ambiguity, no gradual fade — a single decisive image for a single decisive ending.

## Questions for study

1. Why does the merchants' list end on "slaves, and human lives" rather than open with it?
2. What does it mean that the only instruction given is to "come out," not to reform or resist?
3. What do kings, merchants, and sailors have in common in what they grieve — and what's missing from their grief?
