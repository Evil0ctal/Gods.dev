Peter names the objection directly: scoffers are asking "where is the promise of his coming? For, from the day that the fathers fell asleep, all things continue as they were" (3:4). It's a reasonable-sounding complaint — time has passed, nothing dramatic has happened, maybe the promise was wrong. Peter doesn't dismiss the question; he reframes the clock it's being measured on.

## A different measurement of time

Verse 8's claim that "one day is with the Lord as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" isn't a vague mystical statement — it's a direct answer to the delay objection. What reads as slowness on a human calendar isn't slowness at all when measured against God's patience, which Peter names explicitly as reluctance for anyone to perish (3:9).

## Dissolving elements, new heavens

The chapter's vivid picture of the heavens passing away with a roar and the elements melting with fire (3:10-12) is startling, but it serves the same purpose as the rest of the letter — grounding present behavior in future certainty. Because this is coming, Peter asks, "what manner of people ought you to be in holy living and godliness" right now.

> *"The Lord is not slow concerning his promise... but is patient with us, not wishing that anyone should perish."* — 2 Peter 3:9

## Questions for study

1. Where in your own life do you feel the tension between God's promises and the slowness of their fulfillment, as the scoffers describe in verse 4?
2. How does reframing delay as patience (3:9) change your posture toward waiting on something you've been promised?
3. Peter asks what kind of people we ought to be given where history is headed (3:11) — how would you answer that honestly right now?
