The first thing anyone tells you about the man in the tombs is what he cannot be stopped by: chains, shackles, the whole village's best efforts (5:3-4). Mark spends four verses cataloguing a failure before Jesus even speaks, which makes the healing that follows almost anticlimactic — one exchange, and the man just described as unbindable is "clothed, and in his right mind" (5:15).

> Read it in the terminal: `bible mark 5`

## A name that is a crowd

Asked his name, the man answers "Legion, for we are many" (5:9) — a Roman military word, in occupied territory, spoken by a man nobody could subdue. Mark doesn't explain the choice; he just lets it sit there, uncomfortably specific, in a chapter otherwise about private suffering.

## Interrupted on the way

The story that follows braids two together: Jairus begging for his dying daughter, and a hemorrhaging woman who spent everything on doctors and only got worse (5:26). She reaches for the hem of Jesus's robe rather than his attention — healing without the exposure of being noticed. He stops the whole procession for her anyway.

> *"He said to her, 'Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be cured of your disease.'"* — Mark 5:34

## Too late, and then not

By the time Jesus reaches Jairus's house, the girl is dead and mourners have already started wailing. "Talitha koum" — "little girl, get up" — undoes the delay entirely. The chapter's timing runs on one principle throughout: the interruption was never a threat to the outcome.

## Questions for study

1. The man called Legion is a crowd wearing one body. What competing voices make up your own "legion"?
2. The bleeding woman sought healing without being seen. Where do you do the same — want relief but avoid being known?
3. Jesus stops for one woman while a dying girl waits. What does that say about how he weighs interruptions?
4. "Talitha koum" is spoken to someone everyone else had given up on. Who comes to mind?
