It's tempting to skim Romans 16 — it's mostly names, twenty-some of them, most unfamiliar. But a list of names in an ancient letter is a map, and this one maps an entire church Paul has never visited, person by person, before he's even walked in the door.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible romans 16`

## Phoebe, carrying the letter

The chapter opens with a commendation for Phoebe, "a servant of the assembly that is at Cenchreae" (16:1), almost certainly the very person entrusted to carry this letter to Rome and likely read it aloud there. Before anyone's theology gets discussed, a woman's trustworthiness is put on the record first.

## A crowded, ordinary church

What follows is a roll call — Prisca and Aquila, who laid down their own necks for Paul's life (16:3-4), Mary who "labored much" (16:6), Andronicus and Junia, notable among the apostles (16:7), and on through names history otherwise forgot. Paul's churches were not abstractions; they were specific people he could name, thank, and miss.

## A warning tucked at the end

Just before the letter closes, Paul slips in a warning:

> *"Now I beg you, brothers, look out for those who are causing the divisions and occasions of stumbling, contrary to the doctrine which you learned, and turn away from them."* — Romans 16:17

Even in a chapter of affection, he doesn't let vigilance lapse — smooth, flattering speech, he warns, deceives "the hearts of the innocent" (16:18).

## Questions for study

1. Who are the "Phoebes" and "Priscillas" carrying weight in your own community that don't get named often enough?
2. Why might Paul close his most doctrinally dense letter with a list of ordinary names?
3. How do warmth (the greetings) and wariness (the warning) coexist in this one chapter?
