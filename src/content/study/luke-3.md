Luke opens this chapter like a court record: the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Pontius Pilate governing Judea, Herod tetrarch of Galilee, Annas and Caiaphas as high priests. No other Gospel bothers pinning John the Baptist to this much civic detail. It matters — Luke keeps insisting that the gospel happened inside real politics, to real people, not in some timeless myth-space.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 3`

## What then must we do?

John's preaching is fierce — "brood of vipers," an ax already at the root of the trees — but when the crowd asks what repentance actually looks like, his answers are almost dull. Share a coat. Don't overcharge. Don't extort with threats. Repentance here isn't an emotional state; it's a change in how you handle your money and your power over other people.

## A voice, then a dove, then a name

John insists someone greater is coming who will baptize with the Holy Spirit and fire. When Jesus is baptized, the sky opens, the Spirit descends "in a bodily form, as a dove," and a voice names him Son. The scene answers John's preaching directly — this is the one whose sandals John isn't worthy to untie.

## A genealogy that reaches past Abraham

Luke's genealogy, unlike Matthew's, runs backward all the way to "Adam, the son of God." Matthew is arguing Jesus is Israel's king; Luke is arguing something wider — that this birth concerns the whole human family, not one nation's line.

> *"He who has two coats, let him give to him who has none. He who has food, let him do likewise."* — Luke 3:11

> *"All flesh will see God's salvation."* — Luke 3:6

## Questions for study

1. Why does Luke bother dating John's ministry to specific rulers and years?
2. What does it mean that John's answers to "what should we do" are about coats and wages rather than religious rituals?
3. What is the effect of tracing Jesus's genealogy back to Adam rather than stopping at Abraham or David?
4. How does the voice at Jesus's baptism confirm or complicate what John had been announcing?
