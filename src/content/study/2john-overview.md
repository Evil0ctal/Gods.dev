Of the twenty-seven books that make up the New Testament, 2 John is one of the shortest, closer in length to a note than a treatise, and it ends the way a real letter to a real person often does: John says he has more to say but would rather say it face to face than trust it to paper and ink. That detail alone tells you something the theology can't — this is correspondence between people who know each other, not a circular meant for strangers.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 2john 1`

## A small letter carrying a big warning

Despite its size, 2 John covers ground familiar from 1 John: walking in truth, the command to love, and a warning against "many deceivers" who deny Jesus Christ came in the flesh. It's 1 John's theology applied to one specific, practical decision: who do you let teach under your roof?

## Joy as the stated goal

John frames even his boundary-setting as aimed at joy, not merely correctness.

> *"I hope to come to you, and speak face to face, that our joy may be made full."* — 2 John 1:12

## A letter that trusts relationship over paper

There's something worth noticing in a New Testament writer choosing presence over documentation whenever he can. Instruction on paper is a substitute for the real thing, used only because distance requires it.

> *"Grace, mercy, and peace will be with us, from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ."* — 2 John 1:3

## Questions for study

1. What does John choosing to withhold some things for a face-to-face visit tell you about how he saw written instruction?
2. How does this brief letter apply 1 John's larger themes to one very specific situation?
3. What would it look like to treat correctness and joy as goals that belong together rather than in tension?
