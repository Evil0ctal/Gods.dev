Before Paul ever hears "come over into Macedonia and help us" (16:9), Luke records two refusals: "forbidden by the Holy Spirit to speak the word in Asia" (16:6), then blocked again from Bithynia (16:7). Guidance in this chapter looks less like a map and more like a series of closed doors that finally leave one direction open. The vision at Troas isn't the beginning of the guidance — it's the conclusion of it.

## A businesswoman and a slave girl

Once in Philippi, the gospel reaches two very different women. Lydia, a prosperous merchant of purple cloth, has her heart "opened... to listen" (16:14) and offers her home. A nameless slave girl, exploited by owners profiting from her "spirit of divination," is freed by Paul in Jesus's name — and that mercy costs Paul and Silas a beating and a jail cell, because it costs her owners their income (16:16-19).

## Singing in the stocks

At midnight, feet in stocks after a public flogging, Paul and Silas are "praying and singing hymns to God, and the prisoners were listening" (16:25). The earthquake that follows almost feels like an afterthought to the stranger miracle already underway — worship from men with every reason not to.

## What must I do to be saved?

The jailer, about to kill himself over escaped prisoners who never actually left, asks the question the whole book has been building toward: *"Sirs, what must I do to be saved?"* The answer is as plain as the question: *"Believe in the Lord Jesus Christ, and you will be saved, you and your household"* (16:30-31).

## Questions for study

1. How do you tell the difference between a closed door as guidance and a closed door as mere obstacle?
2. Why might freeing the slave girl cost Paul more than any sermon he preached in Philippi?
3. What does it mean that Paul and Silas worshiped before they knew how the story would end?
4. What would it look like to actually believe the jailer's answer applies to "your household" as well as yourself?
