Melchizedek shows up in Genesis just long enough to bless Abraham and receive a tenth of the spoils, then disappears from the record entirely — no father named, no mother, no genealogy, no recorded death. Most readers skim past the gap. The writer of Hebrews builds an entire argument on it: a priesthood that doesn't run through inherited bloodline is a category the Levitical system, tied to genealogy at every point, simply cannot produce.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible hebrews 7`

## The math of the tithe

Levi's whole priesthood collects tithes from the people — but Levi, the writer notes, was still "in the loins of Abraham" when Abraham paid tithes to Melchizedek. The lesser pays tribute to the greater; by that logic, Levi's own priesthood tipped its hat to a superior one before it even existed.

## What the law couldn't finish

> *"The law made nothing perfect, and there is a bringing in thereupon of a better hope."* — Hebrews 7:19 (paraphrased)

The Levitical system required constant renewal because its priests kept dying and needing replacement. The argument turns on permanence: a priest who "remains a priest forever" doesn't need a successor.

## Able to save completely

> *"He is also able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, seeing he lives forever to make intercession for them."* — Hebrews 7:25

## Questions for study

1. Why would a priesthood without a recorded genealogy be more useful for this argument, not less?
2. What's the difference between a mediator who eventually dies and one who "lives forever to make intercession"?
3. Where do you most need an intercessor who never has to be replaced?
