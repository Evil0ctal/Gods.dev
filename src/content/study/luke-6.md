Matthew puts this sermon on a mountainside; Luke puts Jesus "on a level place," down among the crowd he's just healed. It's a small detail with a real effect — this isn't a teacher elevated above the people, it's someone standing where they stand, saying "blessed are you" and "woe to you" in direct address, second person, not the abstract third person of "blessed are the poor."

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 6`

## A blessing that has a woe attached

Every beatitude here has a matching curse: blessed are the poor, woe to the rich; blessed are the hungry, woe to the full; blessed are you who weep, woe to you who laugh now. Luke isn't interested in spiritualizing poverty — the reversal is concrete and economic, and it cuts both directions.

## Love that doesn't wait for reciprocity

"Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you" isn't a general sentiment here — it comes with specifics: bless those who curse you, give without expecting return, lend without collecting. The standard offered isn't fairness but the character of God, who is kind toward the unthankful and evil.

## Two builders, one flood

The chapter ends with a warning dressed as a story: hearing these words and doing them is the only foundation that survives a flood. Everything else, however sincere, collapses in a great ruin.

> *"Blessed are you who are poor, for yours is the Kingdom of God."* — Luke 6:20

> *"Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you."* — Luke 6:27

## Questions for study

1. Why might Luke set this sermon on level ground rather than a mountain?
2. What is uncomfortable about pairing blessings with woes rather than leaving the blessings alone?
3. What makes loving enemies different from merely tolerating them?
4. What "great ruin" have you seen from hearing good teaching without doing it?
