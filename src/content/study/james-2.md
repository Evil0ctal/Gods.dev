James's illustration of favoritism is almost cinematic: two men walk into the assembly, one in a gold ring and fine clothes, one in dirty clothes, and the ushers steer the first to a good seat while telling the second to stand or sit on the floor. He isn't describing a hypothetical. He's daring the reader to admit they've watched this happen, maybe done it themselves.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible james 2`

## The law that makes partiality a sin, not just bad manners

> *"If you fulfill the royal law, according to the Scripture, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself,' you do well."* — James 2:8

By tying favoritism to the love-your-neighbor command, James makes it a violation of law, not a mere social misstep — and notes that breaking the law at one point makes you guilty of the whole thing.

## Faith that never leaves the building

James imagines someone telling a hungry, under-dressed believer "go in peace, be warmed and filled" while giving nothing they actually need. That, he says, is what faith without works looks like — technically present, functionally useless.

> *"Even so faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself."* — James 2:17

## An odd pair of examples

Abraham offering Isaac and Rahab hiding the spies make an unlikely matched set — patriarch and prostitute, both singled out because their faith showed up as action at the decisive moment, not because their résumés matched.

## Questions for study

1. Where have you seen (or done) the kind of seating-preference favoritism James describes?
2. Why does James treat partiality as a law-breaking sin rather than just poor etiquette?
3. What's one place your own faith currently looks more like a sentiment than an action?
