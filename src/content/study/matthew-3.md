John the Baptist does not soften his message for the people who came a long way to hear it. When Pharisees and Sadducees show up at the Jordan, he greets them as "offspring of vipers" (3:7) and tells them their pedigree — "we have Abraham for our father" — won't save them. This is not a warm-up act. Matthew introduces the forerunner as someone who refuses to let religious credentials substitute for actual change.

## Repentance before religion

John's baptism is for repentance, and his central image is agricultural, not sentimental: "the ax already lies at the root of the trees" (3:10), fruit or fire. He is clearing ground for someone greater, and he says so plainly — he is not fit to carry the sandals of the one coming after him (3:11). The forerunner's whole identity is pointing away from himself.

> *"The voice of one crying in the wilderness, 'Make ready the way of the Lord! Make his paths straight!'"* — Matthew 3:3

## The baptism that inverts the pattern

Then the pattern breaks. Jesus comes to be baptized by John, and John objects — he needs it from Jesus, not the other way around (3:14). Jesus insists, "to fulfill all righteousness" (3:15), and the scene ends with heaven opening, the Spirit descending like a dove, and a voice: *"This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased."* The one who needed no cleansing identifies fully with those who do. That is the shape of the whole Gospel in miniature.

## Questions for study

1. John confronted people who assumed their heritage guaranteed them standing with God. What modern equivalents of "we have Abraham for our father" exist?
2. Why does Jesus insist on being baptized when he has no sin to repent of?
3. What does the voice from heaven — spoken before Jesus has done any public ministry — suggest about identity coming before performance?
