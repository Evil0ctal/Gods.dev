King Agrippa gives Paul the floor with a wave of the hand — "You are
permitted to speak for yourself" (26:1) — and Paul delivers what might be
the most polished speech in Acts, aimed at an audience of essentially
one interested man. Midway through, Festus shouts that Paul's learning
has driven him mad (26:24). Paul's reply is calm and direct: "I am not
crazy, most excellent Festus, but boldly declare words of truth and
soberness" (26:25).

## The third telling

This is Paul's Damascus road story for the third time in Acts, and here
it's shaped for a king — more literary, more allusive to Israel's
prophets, ending with Jesus' commission to "open their eyes, that they
may turn from darkness to light" (26:18). Three tellings, three
audiences, three emphases. The event doesn't change; the telling adapts
to who's listening.

## A question that answers itself

Paul turns from narrative to direct address: "King Agrippa, do you
believe the prophets? I know that you believe" (26:27). It's a rhetorical
move built from respect, not manipulation — Paul is naming what Agrippa
already knows to be true about himself, and daring him to follow it to
its conclusion.

> *"Agrippa said to Paul, 'With a little persuasion are you trying to
> make me a Christian?' Paul said, '... not only you, but also all who
> hear me today, might become such as I am, except for these bonds.'"*
> — Acts 26:28-29

## Almost, and the whole distance in that word

Agrippa's response — often rendered "almost you persuade me" — is the
chapter's real tragedy. He is close enough to see it, honest enough to
admit it, and still doesn't cross over. Paul's answer contains no
irritation, only longing: he wishes the same freedom for everyone in the
room, chains excepted. "Almost" persuaded turns out to be the same
distance as not persuaded at all.

## Questions for study

1. Why might the same testimony land differently depending on the
   audience, without Paul changing what actually happened?
2. What is the difference between being intellectually convinced and
   being changed, and where does Agrippa land on that line?
3. Paul answers mockery (26:24) with calm reasoning rather than
   defensiveness. What made that possible for him?
4. Where in your own life have you been "almost persuaded" of something
   you know is true, and what's kept you on that side of the line?
