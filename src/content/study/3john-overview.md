Read straight through, 3 John contains almost no theology at all — no argument about the incarnation, no discussion of sin or forgiveness, nothing you'd put in a doctrinal statement. It's a personal letter to a man named Gaius about a single practical dispute: whether traveling missionaries get welcomed or turned away. That absence is itself instructive. Sometimes the truth of the gospel shows up not in what people say they believe but in who they'll open their door to.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 3john 1`

## A wish before the business

John opens by hoping Gaius prospers in health "even as your soul prospers" (v.2) — treating physical well-being and spiritual health as connected but not identical, worth naming both.

## Three men, one small church

The letter's real content is a triangle: Gaius, generous and faithful; Diotrephes, a local leader who loves preeminence and expels dissenters; Demetrius, well-regarded by everyone. John doesn't theorize about church authority in the abstract — he just describes what happened when one man grabbed for control.

> *"I have no greater joy than this: to hear about my children walking in truth."* — 3 John 1:4

## Presence over paper, again

Like its companion letter, 3 John ends wanting a face-to-face conversation rather than more writing, a small but consistent preference across both short letters.

## Questions for study

1. What does it tell you that a letter with almost no doctrine still counts as Scripture?
2. How does Diotrephes' behavior show that church problems aren't always about false teaching?
3. Where in your own community does hospitality function as a real test of what people believe?
