A woman breaks open a jar of expensive perfume and pours it on Jesus' head; the disciples call it waste (26:8-9). Jesus defends her without qualification: *"she has done a good work for me"* (26:10) and promises her act will be told "wherever this Good News is preached in the whole world" (26:13). It's a strange thing to memorialize forever — not a sermon, not a miracle, just one extravagant, impractical gesture of love, done while there was still time to do it.

## A meal that becomes a promise

At Passover, Jesus takes bread and wine and reframes them: *"Take, eat; this is my body"* and *"this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins"* (26:26-28). He takes an ancient ritual commemorating a past rescue and quietly makes it about a rescue not yet finished.

## The prayer that costs him something real

In Gethsemane, Jesus is "exceedingly sorrowful, even to death" (26:38) and prays three times, honestly: *"My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire"* (26:39). It's not a scripted resignation — the request and the surrender are both real, side by side.

## A kiss, a sword, and a silence

Judas' betrayal-kiss, Peter's sword swing and Jesus' rebuke of it, the disciples scattering, Peter's three denials by a fire — the chapter ends in fragments, everyone failing in a different way, while Jesus alone moves toward what's coming with open eyes.

## Questions for study

1. What would an "impractical" act of love, done now while there's time, look like for you?
2. How does the Lord's Supper change if you read it as Jesus reframing an old rescue around a new one?
3. Gethsemane holds honest reluctance and full surrender together. Which do you tend to skip?
4. Peter's denial follows his loudest promise of loyalty (26:33-35). What's the gap between your promises and your pressure points?
