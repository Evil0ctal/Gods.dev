Pharisees and scribes grumble that Jesus welcomes sinners and eats with them. He doesn't argue the point directly — he tells three stories back to back, each about losing something and throwing a party once it's found: a sheep, a coin, a son. The repetition itself is the argument. This isn't one soft parable balanced against harder teaching elsewhere; it's Jesus saying the same thing three times because apparently it needed saying three times.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 15`

## Ninety-nine isn't enough

The shepherd with a hundred sheep leaves the ninety-nine to go after the one that's missing — not because the ninety-nine don't matter, but because completeness matters to him. The math looks reckless from outside; from inside it's just what having something lost feels like.

## A rehearsed apology, cut short

The younger son practices his speech on the way home — "I am no longer worthy to be called your son" — but his father is already running to him before he can finish it. The apology isn't rejected; it's just interrupted by something faster than justice.

## The brother who stays outside

The parable doesn't end with the party. It ends with the older brother, furious and unmoved by the argument that his brother "was lost, and is found," standing outside a house he grew up in.

> *"While he was still far off, his father saw him, and was moved with compassion, and ran."* — Luke 15:20

> *"There will be more joy in heaven over one sinner who repents, than over ninety-nine righteous people who need no repentance."* — Luke 15:7

## Questions for study

1. Why tell three parables about the same idea instead of just one?
2. What does the shepherd's math suggest about how God weighs the one against the many?
3. Why does the father interrupt his son's rehearsed apology?
4. Is the older brother wrong to be angry, or wrong about something else?
