James traces conflict back further than most people want to go. Not to circumstances, not to the other person, but to unsatisfied desire inside the one doing the fighting: "don't wars and fightings among you come from your pleasures that war in your members?" You want and don't have, so you fight. Even prayer gets recruited into the same pattern — asking and not receiving, because the asking itself is aimed at consuming it on pleasure.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible james 4`

## Friendship with the world as enmity

James doesn't soften the stakes: friendship with the world's values is described flatly as enmity with God, not a neutral lifestyle choice. Wanting what the world wants pulls in a direction opposite to the one that direction claims to be harmless.

## The posture that actually works

> *"Be subject therefore to God. But resist the devil, and he will flee from you."* — James 4:7
> *"Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you."* — James 4:8

Submission and resistance and nearness aren't three separate strategies — they're one posture, described from three angles.

## A warning about presuming on tomorrow

The chapter's closing turn feels almost unrelated but isn't: people confidently planning next year's business trip and next year's profit, as though tomorrow were owed to them.

> *"You don't know what your life will be like tomorrow. For you are a vapor, that appears for a little time, and then vanishes away."* — James 4:14

## Questions for study

1. Where has unsatisfied wanting, rather than someone else's fault, been the real source of a recent conflict of yours?
2. What would it look like this week to "resist the devil" as a specific action rather than a vague intention?
3. How does remembering life is "a vapor" change how you're currently planning ahead?
