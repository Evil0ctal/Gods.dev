Notice what's missing at the start of Acts 13: no missions committee, no fundraising plan, no strategic rationale recorded. Leaders in Antioch are simply worshiping and fasting when "the Holy Spirit said, 'Separate Barnabas and Saul for me, for the work to which I have called them'" (13:2). The most consequential missionary journey in the New Testament starts as an interruption to a prayer meeting, not the output of one.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible acts 13`

## A sorcerer, blinded

Early on Cyprus, a magician named Elymas tries to talk a Roman official out of the faith. Paul's response is severe — Elymas is struck blind on the spot (13:11) — a strange, almost Old Testament-style judgment that seems designed less to punish than to make the point publicly undeniable.

## A sermon that retells everything

In the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, Paul does what Stephen did before him: retells Israel's story, this time landing it squarely on Jesus's resurrection and a justification "from all things" the law of Moses could never provide (13:38-39). It's the same method — history as argument — put to a different use.

## We turn to the Gentiles

Rejected by the synagogue crowd but embraced by Gentile listeners, Paul and Barnabas make their boldest statement yet: *"since indeed you thrust it from yourselves... behold, we turn to the Gentiles"* (13:46). It's not spite; it's a hinge the whole book has been turning toward since Cornelius.

## Questions for study

1. What's significant about a global mission beginning inside an act of worship rather than a plan?
2. Why might Paul's judgment on Elymas function differently than punishment for its own sake?
3. How does Paul's synagogue sermon reuse Stephen's approach from chapter 7, and to what end?
4. What does "we turn to the Gentiles" cost Paul and Barnabas, and what does it open up?
