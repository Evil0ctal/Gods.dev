The disciples open the chapter with a question that assumes suffering is always somebody's fault: who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind? Jesus rejects the premise entirely and heals him anyway, using mud and saliva and a walk to the pool of Siloam, an oddly physical, almost inefficient method for someone who elsewhere heals with a single word.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible john 9`

## Wrong question, real healing
The theology of the disciples' question turns out to matter less than what Jesus actually does about the suffering in front of him.

## Interrogated for being healed
What follows is almost comic in its escalation: neighbors, then Pharisees, then the man's own parents, afraid of being expelled from the synagogue, all get pulled into interrogating a man whose only offense is being able to see. He doesn't have a theological argument ready. He just keeps repeating what happened to him.

> *"One thing I know: that though I was blind, now I see."* — John 9:25

## Blindness that sees itself
Jesus closes the episode by turning the whole premise upside down: the man born blind sees, and the men who claim to see clearly are the ones actually blind.

> *"Those who don't see may see, and those who see may become blind."* — John 9:39

## Questions for study
1. Why does Jesus reject the disciples' assumption that suffering equals someone's specific sin?
2. What does the man's simple testimony, "I was blind, now I see," accomplish that a theological argument couldn't?
3. Why does interrogating a healed man become more urgent to the Pharisees than the healing itself?
4. In what way might claiming to already see clearly be its own kind of blindness?
