Jesus gives the twelve real authority — over unclean spirits, disease, sickness of every kind (10:1) — and then sends them out with almost nothing: no gold, no silver, no extra tunic, no staff (10:9-10). The power and the poverty arrive together. This isn't an accident of the text; it's the whole model of the mission. They are meant to depend on the hospitality of the towns they enter, not on their own resourcing.

## Sheep among wolves

Jesus doesn't oversell the assignment. He tells them plainly they're being sent out "as sheep among wolves" and to be "wise as serpents, and harmless as doves" (10:16) — a strange combination of shrewdness and innocence, neither naive nor predatory. He warns of councils, floggings, betrayal by family members, and hatred "for my name's sake" (10:22). This is recruitment language that includes the casualty report up front.

> *"Behold, I send you out as sheep among wolves. Be therefore as wise as serpents, and harmless as doves."* — Matthew 10:16

## Losing your life to find it

The chapter's hardest line comes near the end: Jesus says he came not to bring peace but a sword, setting even family members against each other over allegiance to him (10:34-36). Then the paradox that runs through the whole New Testament shows up in miniature: whoever tries to keep their life will lose it, and whoever loses it for Jesus' sake will find it (10:39). Discipleship here is framed less as an addition to your existing life and more as a complete reordering of what you're willing to lose.

## Questions for study

1. Why send the disciples out with authority but almost no material resources — what does that combination train them for?
2. "Wise as serpents, harmless as doves" resists both naivety and cynicism. Which side do you tend to drift toward?
3. What would it cost a modern reader — concretely, not abstractly — to take 10:39 at face value?
