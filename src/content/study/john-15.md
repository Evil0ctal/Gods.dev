Jesus and the disciples may well have been walking past actual vineyards on their way out of Jerusalem when he says it: *"I am the true vine, and my Father is the farmer"* (15:1). It's an image his listeners had seen every harvest — pruned branches piled for burning, fruitful ones tied up and thriving. He applies it to himself without softening the stakes.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible john 15`

## Apart from me, nothing

The chapter's engine is one small verb, repeated: *remain* in me. Not visit, not admire from a distance — remain. *"He who remains in me and I in him bears much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing"* (15:5). That last clause is total, not partial. Branches don't negotiate fruit on their own terms.

## Greater love

In the middle of talk about commandments and obedience, Jesus defines love by its ceiling, not its floor:

> *"Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends."* — John 15:13

He says it before he does it. By the end of the week, the definition and the deed match.

## Friends, not just servants

He upgrades the disciples' status mid-chapter — no longer servants who don't know their master's business, but friends who've been told everything he heard from the Father (15:15). Then, almost in the same breath, he warns them the world will hate them exactly as it hated him (15:18-20). Friendship with Jesus was never advertised as friendship with popularity.

## Questions for study

1. What does "remaining" in Jesus look like on an ordinary Tuesday, as opposed to a crisis?
2. Verse 5 claims you can do "nothing" apart from him — what's your honest reaction to that word?
3. How does being called "friend" rather than "servant" change your sense of the relationship?
4. Jesus pairs love with a warning of hatred in the same breath. Why might those two belong together?
