Eutychus falls asleep during a sermon and falls out a window. Luke reports
it with almost no drama — a young man named Eutychus, sitting in the
window, sinks into deep sleep as Paul keeps talking, and "fell down from
the third story, and was taken up dead" (20:9). It is a strange, human
detail to keep in a book about the Spirit's advance: even Paul's preaching
put someone to sleep.

## An unglamorous miracle

Paul goes down, embraces the boy, and says, "Don't be troubled, for his
life is in him." No fanfare, no crowd chanting — just a man doing what
needed doing, then going back upstairs to keep talking until daybreak. The
chapter's first miracle is almost a footnote to the meeting it interrupts.

## The speech at Miletus

The center of the chapter is Paul's address to the Ephesian elders
(20:17-35) — the only speech in Acts given to Christians rather than
outsiders, and it reads like a man settling his accounts. He reviews his
record ("I didn't shrink from declaring to you anything that was
profitable"), warns of wolves who will rise from their own number
(20:29-30), and commends them to God rather than to a successor. There is
no institutional handoff here, no ordained replacement named. Paul trusts
the word, not the org chart.

## Bound in the Spirit, going anyway

Twice Paul says he is compelled toward Jerusalem "not knowing the things
that will happen to me there," except that in every city the Spirit
testifies that chains and afflictions wait for him (20:22-23). That is
the chapter's quiet center: obedience that walks toward its own cost with
open eyes.

> *"But I hold my life of no account as dear to myself, so that I may
> finish my race with joy, and the ministry which I received from the
> Lord Jesus."* — Acts 20:24

## Questions for study

1. Paul entrusts the church to God's word rather than to a hierarchy —
   what does that say about how he understood leadership?
2. The Eutychus incident is treated almost casually. Why do you think
   Luke includes it without more fanfare?
3. "More blessed to give than to receive" (20:35) is quoted nowhere else
   in the Gospels — what makes Paul cite it here?
4. What would it look like to hold your own life "of no account" while
   still doing careful, patient work?
