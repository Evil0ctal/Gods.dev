After the drama of Philippi and the mixed reception in Athens, Corinth is almost anticlimactic — and that's the point. Paul finds a Jewish couple, Aquila and Priscilla, and "because he practiced the same trade, he lived with them and worked, for by trade they were tent makers" (18:3). No riot, no council hearing, just eighteen months of teaching and stitching leather, the longest settled stretch of his ministry so far.

## A promise to stay put

What keeps Paul in Corinth isn't comfort but a direct word: *"Don't be afraid, but speak and don't be silent; for I am with you, and no one will attack you to harm you, for I have many people in this city"* (18:9-10). God had a congregation there before Paul had preached a single sermon to gather it.

## A judge who won't judge

When accusers finally do drag Paul before the Roman proconsul Gallio, the case collapses on procedure: Gallio dismisses it as an internal religious dispute beneath his court's concern (18:14-15). It's an unglamorous kind of protection — not vindication, just official indifference, which turns out to be enough.

## Filling in the gaps

The chapter closes on Apollos, "an eloquent man... mighty in the Scriptures," who nonetheless knows only John's baptism. Priscilla and Aquila, rather than publicly correcting him, "took him aside, and explained to him the way of God more accurately" (18:26). Gifted people can still have gaps, and correcting them privately is itself a ministry.

## Questions for study

1. Why might Paul's quietest, longest stay be as significant as his most dramatic confrontations?
2. What does it mean that God said he had "many people" in Corinth before anyone had believed yet?
3. How does Gallio's indifference function as a kind of providence?
4. What does Priscilla and Aquila's private correction of Apollos teach about how to handle someone else's blind spot?
