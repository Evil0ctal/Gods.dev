Read this chapter looking for the qualification you'd expect — talent, vision, charisma — and you won't find it. Paul's list for an overseer is almost entirely about temperament and household: temperate, sensible, modest, not quarrelsome, one who rules his own house well (3:2-4). The job description is boring on purpose.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible 1timothy 3`

## Not a novice

One line stands out for what it forbids rather than requires: "not a new convert, lest being puffed up he fall into the same condemnation as the devil" (3:6). Paul isn't worried about ability. He's worried about pride arriving before maturity has had time to catch up with position.

## The test extended to deacons

The same scrutiny is applied to deacons — grave, not double-tongued, tested first (3:8-10) — and to their households, refusing to separate a person's public role from their private life. Character, in this chapter, is assumed to be one thing, not two.

## The mystery underneath the job description

The chapter ends somewhere unexpected — a burst of hymn-like praise about Christ: revealed in the flesh, justified in the spirit, received up in glory (3:16). All that dull, careful qualification-checking exists to protect something this astonishing. The plumbing is boring so the water can be clean.

> *"God was revealed in the flesh, justified in the spirit... received up in glory."* — 1 Timothy 3:16

## Questions for study

1. Where have you, or a leader you know, mistaken talent for the qualifications Paul actually lists here?
2. Why do you think Paul worries more about a new convert's pride than their lack of experience?
3. "Tested first" — what would testing before trusting look like in your own community?
4. How does the closing hymn (3:16) change your read of the careful, unglamorous list before it?
