Jesus is tried three times in this chapter — before the council, before Pilate, before Herod — and the verdict is the same each time, even though nobody says it plainly: no actual crime. Pilate says it outright, twice. Herod, who wanted a magic show, sends him back with nothing more than mockery and a purple robe. The trials aren't really about evidence. They're about a decision already made, looking for a process to justify it.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible luke 23`

## No fault found, three times over
Pilate's own words undercut the entire proceeding against Jesus, but he hands him over anyway rather than face down the crowd.

## Forgiveness from the cross
Nailed up, in pain, Jesus's first recorded words aren't self-defense or despair.

> *"Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing."* — Luke 23:34

## Two criminals, one answer
Of the two men crucified alongside him, one hurls insults; the other admits he's getting what he deserves and simply asks to be remembered. Jesus doesn't ask him for a longer resume of faith. He answers immediately, in the present tense.

> *"Assuredly I tell you, today you will be with me in Paradise."* — Luke 23:43

## A tomb that wasn't his
Joseph of Arimathea, a council member who hadn't consented to the plan against Jesus, gives him his own new tomb. Even the burial is an act of quiet dissent.

## Questions for study
1. What does it mean that three separate authorities all found "no fault" in Jesus, yet condemned him anyway?
2. Why does Jesus answer the second criminal so quickly, without any test of sincerity?
3. What does Joseph of Arimathea's action suggest about hidden dissent within the council?
4. What would it cost you to ask, like the second criminal, simply to be remembered?
