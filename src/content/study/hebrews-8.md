The writer's clinching argument for a new covenant is almost linguistic. He quotes Jeremiah, writing centuries after Sinai and long before Jesus, promising a covenant God would one day make that wasn't like the one made with Israel's ancestors. The single word "new" is doing enormous work here: even under the old system, God's own prophets already understood the arrangement as provisional, not final.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible hebrews 8`

## A covenant written on the inside

The Jeremiah quotation is long and specific, and its center is a relocation: law no longer written on stone tablets outside a person, but on mind and heart.

> *"I will put my laws into their mind, I will also write them on their heart... I will be merciful to their unrighteousness."* — Hebrews 8:10-12 (paraphrased)

That's not a tweak to the old system. It's a description of a different kind of obedience altogether — internal rather than externally imposed.

## Old by definition

> *"In that he says, 'A new covenant,' he has made the first old. Now that which is becoming old and grows aged is near to vanishing away."* — Hebrews 8:13

The writer isn't arguing that the old covenant failed through some defect in its design; he's arguing that its own prophetic literature announced its expiration date in advance.

## A minister of the true sanctuary

Jesus is described as minister of the true tabernacle, the one the earthly version was only ever a copy of — priesthood and covenant both upgraded together, not separately.

## Questions for study

1. Why might God announcing the old covenant's obsolescence through the prophets matter more than announcing it through Jesus alone?
2. What's the practical difference between law written on stone and law written on the heart?
3. Where in your own life is obedience still more external than internal?
