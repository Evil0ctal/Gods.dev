Jesus tells the disciples something that sounds, on its face, like bad news dressed up as good: *"it is to your advantage that I go away"* (16:7). They've spent three years learning to need him in the room. Now he's telling them his absence will actually work in their favor, because it makes room for the Counselor.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible john 16`

## Sorrow with an expiration date

Jesus reaches for an image every parent in the room would recognize — labor. *"A woman, when she gives birth, has sorrow because her time has come. But when she has delivered the child, she doesn't remember the anguish any more, for the joy"* (16:21). He's not denying the disciples' coming grief; he's telling them it has a shape, a beginning and an end, with joy waiting on the other side.

## Plain speech, at last

The disciples finally admit they understand him — *"Now you speak plainly, and no figure of speech"* (16:29) — right before he predicts they'll scatter within hours and leave him alone (16:32). Understanding the words and having the courage to act on them turn out to be two different things.

## Overcome

The chapter's last word is the one worth keeping:

> *"In the world you have trouble; but cheer up! I have overcome the world."* — John 16:33

Not "you will avoid trouble" — trouble is guaranteed. The claim is about who has already won underneath it.

## Questions for study

1. What would it look like to believe Jesus' absence is "to your advantage" in a season you're currently in?
2. The labor-pain image gives grief a shape and an end. Does that change how you're carrying a current sorrow?
3. The disciples understood the words and still scattered. What's the gap between comprehension and courage in your own life?
4. "I have overcome the world" is a claim made before the cross. What does it cost Jesus to say it in advance?
