A mob has just tried to kill Paul. Roman soldiers have pulled him out and
are about to flog him for questioning. And Paul's response, given the
chance to speak, is not a defense of his rights or an accusation against
his attackers — it's a story. "Brothers and fathers, listen to the
defense which I now make to you," he says, and then just tells them what
happened to him (22:1).

## Common ground before the hard part

Paul opens in Hebrew (or Aramaic), establishing that he is one of them: a
Jew, trained under Gamaliel, "zealous for God" — and a persecutor of "the
Way" to the point of imprisoning and killing its people (22:3-5). He
doesn't soften his old violence to make himself look better. He names it
plainly before he tells them what stopped it.

## The road, again

This is the second of three tellings of Paul's conversion in Acts, and
the details shift slightly each time — evidence, if anything, that Luke
is recording testimony rather than polishing a legend. What stays
constant is the light, the voice, and the question: "Saul, Saul, why do
you persecute me?" (22:7-8). Notice that Jesus doesn't ask why Paul
persecutes the church. He says *me*. The line between Christ and his
people is, in this account, no line at all.

> *"Now why do you wait? Arise, be baptized, and wash away your sins,
> calling on the name of the Lord."* — Acts 22:16

## One word that ends the hearing

Everything holds until Paul repeats a single word Jesus told him:
"Gentiles." At that word, the crowd that had listened all the way through
his testimony erupts — "Away with such a fellow from the earth!" (22:22).
The scandal was never that Paul changed sides. It was that God's mercy
had a wider address than they were willing to accept.

## Questions for study

1. Paul names his own violent past without excusing it. What does that
   cost him, and what does it buy him with this audience?
2. Why might the same testimony be received calmly for twenty verses and
   then explode at one word?
3. What is the "Gentiles" word in your own context — the point where
   grace for others becomes harder to accept than grace for yourself?
4. Compare this account with Acts 9. What does Paul emphasize here that
   Luke's narration didn't?
