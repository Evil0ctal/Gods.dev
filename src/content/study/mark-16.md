Three women walk to the tomb worrying about a practical problem — who will roll away the stone (16:3) — and arrive to find the problem already solved, the stone moved, and the body gone. Mark's earliest and best manuscripts end the book on a note that has unsettled readers for centuries: the women flee in fear and "said nothing to anyone" (16:8). A Gospel about news that travels ends, structurally, on a sentence about silence.

## He is not here

The messenger at the tomb doesn't soften the shock; he states it and points to the evidence:

> *"He said to them, 'Don't be amazed. You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified. He has risen! He is not here. Behold, the place where they laid him!'"* — Mark 16:6

The instruction that follows sends the women back to Galilee — back to where the Gospel began. The resurrection reopens the story rather than simply closing it.

## An ending that isn't quite one

Whatever you make of the longer endings later scribes attached to this chapter, the earliest text stops at verse 8, on fear and silence, with the reader left holding an announcement the women couldn't yet voice. It's an odd way to end good news, unless the whole point is that the story was never Mark's alone to finish — it's the reader's.

## Questions for study

1. The women worry about a stone that turns out to already be handled. What "stone" are you currently worrying over that may already be moved?
2. Fear and silence are the first response to resurrection in this account, not celebration. Does that surprise you?
3. The messenger sends the women back to Galilee — back to the beginning. Why might resurrection point backward before it points forward?
4. If Mark's Gospel ends with the story unfinished, what does it look like for you to pick up where the silence left off?
