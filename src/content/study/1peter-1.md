Peter addresses this letter to "those who are living as foreigners, scattered" (1:1) — people who have already lost the stability of home, and he doesn't try to talk them out of that instability. Instead he stacks up things that can't be taken: an inheritance that doesn't fade (1:4), a faith more precious than gold refined by fire (1:7), a salvation the prophets themselves searched to understand (1:10-12). The letter's opening move is to relocate security somewhere loss can't reach it.

## Grief that coexists with joy

Peter doesn't tell his readers their suffering isn't real or that grief is a faith failure. Verse 6 holds both at once — "in this you greatly rejoice, though now for a little while, if need be, you have been put to grief in various trials." The joy isn't a replacement for the grief; it runs alongside it, aimed at something the grief can't touch.

## Holiness as an inherited command

Chapter 1 closes by tying holy living directly to God's own character — "you shall be holy, for I am holy" (1:16), borrowed straight from Leviticus. Peter isn't inventing a new ethic for hard times; he's telling a suffering, displaced people that the ancient call to reflect God's character still applies, maybe more urgently, precisely when it's hardest to see the point.

> *"You were redeemed... with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish or spot, the blood of Christ."* — 1 Peter 1:18-19

## Questions for study

1. What in your own life currently feels unstable, and how does Peter's language of an "inheritance that doesn't fade" speak to it?
2. Can you hold grief and rejoicing together the way verse 6 describes, or does one usually cancel the other for you?
3. What would it look like this week to let holiness be a response to who God is (1:16) rather than a performance for other people?
