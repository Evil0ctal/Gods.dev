A man lame from birth is carried to the temple gate every day to beg — a fixture of the landscape, the kind of person crowds learn to walk past without seeing. Peter and John stop. "Peter, fastening his eyes on him... said, 'Look at us'" (3:4). The healing that follows starts with something smaller and rarer than a miracle: attention.

## No silver, something else

Peter is upfront about what he doesn't have: *"Silver and gold I don't have, but what I have, that I give you. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!"* (3:6). He takes the man by the hand, and strength floods into feet and ankles that had never held weight. The man doesn't just walk — he goes into the temple "walking, leaping, and praising God" (3:8).

## The sermon after the sign

A crowd gathers, amazed, and Peter immediately redirects their astonishment away from himself: *"Why do you fasten your eyes on us, as though by our own power or godliness we had made him walk?"* (3:12). The miracle is the hook; the sermon is the point — another call to recognize the Jesus they handed over to Pilate.

## Times of refreshing

Peter's appeal ends with an image worth lingering on: *"Repent therefore, and turn again, that your sins may be blotted out, so that there may come times of refreshing from the presence of the Lord"* (3:19-20). Repentance here isn't framed as punishment avoided but as refreshment arriving.

## Questions for study

1. Peter's first act is attention — "Look at us" — before any miracle happens. What would it cost you to actually see the people you usually walk past?
2. "Such as I have, I give you" — what do you actually have to offer, minus the things you wish you had instead?
3. Peter deflects credit immediately after the miracle. What guards against taking credit for what God does through you?
4. How does "times of refreshing" reframe repentance compared to how you usually think about it?
