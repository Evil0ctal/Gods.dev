Chapter 9 reads oddly at first — Paul builds an airtight case that apostles have every right to financial support (soldiers get wages, farmers eat the grapes they plant, even the Old Testament law protects the working ox), and then announces he hasn't used any of it. "I have used none of these things," he says flatly (9:15), and the rest of the chapter explains why a right you're entitled to isn't always a right you should exercise.

## Becoming all things

> *"I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some."* — 1 Corinthians 9:22

This line gets quoted as flexibility for its own sake, but the context is narrower and harder: Paul is describing self-limitation for the sake of removing obstacles to the gospel. It costs him something specific each time — his freedom, his preferences, his rightful support.

## Training, not drifting

The athletic imagery in 9:24-27 is deliberately unglamorous. Runners train for a prize that fades; Paul trains for one that doesn't. "I beat my body and bring it into submission" isn't self-loathing — it's the discipline of someone who has watched people preach to others and disqualify themselves.

## The unpaid preacher

Paul's refusal to take money wasn't a universal rule for ministers (he defends the opposite practice earlier in the chapter) — it was a specific choice to avoid any suspicion that his gospel was a transaction. Sometimes the way to prove something is free is to refuse payment for delivering it.

## Questions for study

1. What right do you hold that, if surrendered, would remove an obstacle for someone else?
2. Where does "becoming all things to all people" risk becoming people-pleasing instead of service — how do you tell the difference?
3. What are you currently in training for, and does your discipline match the goal?
