An angel comes down "having the key of the abyss and a great chain in his hand" (20:1) and that's the whole arsenal required to bind the dragon for a thousand years. After chapters of war, beasts, and cosmic-scale destruction, the dragon's captivity is handled with a key and a chain — anticlimactic on purpose. The threat that seemed to require an army is neutralized by one angel doing ordinary work.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible revelation 20`

## A resurrection with a number attached

> *"Blessed and holy is he who has part in the first resurrection. Over these, the second death has no power."* — Revelation 20:6

The chapter names two resurrections and two deaths, refusing to let either word stand as a vague spiritual metaphor. It wants precision here, not atmosphere.

## Released, not defeated permanently — yet

After the thousand years, the dragon is "released for a short time" and immediately goes back to deceiving nations (20:3, 7-8). Even a millennium of captivity doesn't change his character; he comes out doing exactly what he was doing before he went in.

## Books, and one more book

At the great white throne, the dead are judged "according to their works" from books that were kept — and then "another book was opened, which is the book of life" (20:12). Works are recorded in detail; belonging is recorded in a separate book entirely.

## Questions for study

1. Why might the dragon's imprisonment be described in such ordinary, unglamorous terms (a key, a chain)?
2. What does the dragon's identical behavior after release suggest about whether captivity can change character?
3. Why keep works and the book of life as two separate records rather than one?
