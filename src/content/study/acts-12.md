Acts 12 opens with a brutal asymmetry Luke doesn't try to smooth over. Herod kills James with the sword in a single sentence (12:2), then arrests Peter intending the same fate — and Peter gets an angel, open gates, and a walk to freedom. No formula explains why one apostle dies and the other is delivered. The chapter simply lets both realities stand side by side.

## Praying anyway

Between James's death and Peter's arrest sits one plain sentence: "constant prayer was made by the assembly to God for him" (12:5). The church doesn't know how the story ends. They pray the same whether the answer will be a miracle or a martyrdom.

## Too excited to open the door

The prison-break scene is famously specific and almost comic: Peter, freed by an angel, knocks at Mary's door, and the servant girl Rhoda is so overjoyed she runs to tell everyone — leaving Peter standing outside (12:14). The believers inside, who had just spent a night praying for exactly this, don't believe it's really him. Answered prayer can catch even the ones praying it off guard.

## The worm and the throne

Herod's end closes the chapter as sharply as it opened: accepting a crowd's cry of "the voice of a god, and not of a man," he is struck down and "eaten by worms" (12:22-23). Then one line resets the scale of the whole episode: "But the word of God grew and multiplied" (12:24). Kings rise and fall; the word keeps moving.

## Questions for study

1. Why do you think Luke places James's death and Peter's rescue in the same chapter without comment?
2. What does the church's reaction to Rhoda's news say about the difference between praying and expecting?
3. What is the danger in Herod's willingness to accept worship as a god?
4. How does 12:24 reframe everything that happened earlier in the chapter?
