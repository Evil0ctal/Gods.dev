Romans 13 opens with the verses that get quoted in every argument about Christians and government — submit to the governing authorities, because there is no authority except from God (13:1). It's a hard passage to read simply, written by a man who would eventually be executed by one of those authorities. Paul isn't handing rulers a blank check; he's describing a general order, not blessing every specific act of it.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible romans 13`

## An order, not an idol

Government exists, in Paul's framing, to praise good conduct and restrain evil (13:3-4) — a real but limited function. The chapter never says "obey no matter what"; it says structure itself is part of how God restrains chaos. Reading past verse 7 without slowing down misses where the chapter is actually headed.

## The debt you can't pay off

Verse 8 pivots hard: "Owe no one anything, except to love one another." Every other debt in the chapter — taxes, respect, honor — gets a number and gets paid. Love is the one line item Paul deliberately leaves open, because it's designed to never close out. You can pay your taxes in full. You cannot finish loving your neighbor.

## Love as the whole law, compressed

> *"Love doesn't harm a neighbor. Love therefore is the fulfillment of the law."* — Romans 13:10

The chapter ends looking toward morning — "the night is far gone, and the day is near" (13:12) — urgency dressed as ethics. Live like the sun's already coming up, because it is.

## Questions for study

1. Where do you draw the line between "submit to authority" and "authority isn't ultimate"?
2. What would it look like to treat love as a debt that's never fully paid, rather than a feeling you either have or don't?
3. Does "the day is near" change the urgency of how you're living right now?
