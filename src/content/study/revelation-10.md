An angel hands John a scroll and tells him to eat it, and the taste comes with a warning attached in advance: sweet in the mouth, bitter in the stomach (10:9-10). It's a strange way to deliver a message, but it's exactly right for what this chapter is about — receiving the word of God is not a single sensation. It changes as it settles.

## What John isn't allowed to write

Seven thunders speak, and John moves to record what they said — then he's stopped and told to seal up what they uttered rather than write it down (10:4). Revelation is famous for unveiling; here it deliberately withholds. Not everything shown is meant to be repeated. Some things stay between the seer and the source.

## No more delay

The angel raises a hand toward heaven and swears an oath: there will be no more delay, and the mystery of God will be finished when the seventh trumpet sounds. After chapters of horror unfolding in stages, this is a small, sturdy promise — the story is going somewhere, and it's going there on schedule.

## Sweet, then bitter

> *"Take it, and eat it up. It will make your stomach bitter, but in your mouth it will be as sweet as honey."* — Revelation 10:9-10

John eats the scroll and is then told he must prophesy again — the message doesn't end with him privately digesting it. It has to go back out, undigested sweetness turned into something he now has to carry and speak, bitterness included.

## Questions for study

1. Why might some revelation be sealed rather than shared, even in a book about unveiling?
2. What does the sweet-then-bitter scroll suggest about the difference between hearing truth and living with it?
3. Where in your own life has a truth been sweet to receive and harder to carry?
