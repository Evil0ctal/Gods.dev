The writer just finished quoting God's verdict on the wilderness generation — they would not enter his rest — and now asks a sharp follow-up question: if Joshua had actually led Israel into that rest for good, why would God, centuries later through David, still be saying "today, if you hear his voice"? The word "today" surviving that long is the writer's evidence that the promise outlived its first, failed attempt.

> Read it in the terminal: `bible hebrews 4`

## A rest still on offer

Since the disobedient generation didn't enter, and later generations were still being invited, the rest remains open — which means the urgency isn't a historical footnote. It's addressed to whoever's reading.

## A word with a blade

> *"For the word of God is living, and active, and sharper than any two-edged sword... and able to discern the thoughts and intentions of the heart."* — Hebrews 4:12

This verse gets quoted as a general statement about Scripture's power, but in context it's specifically about the exposure that comes right before entering rest — nothing about you stays hidden going in.

## A priest who has felt it

The chapter's resolution is unexpectedly tender: a high priest who can sympathize with weakness, having been tested in every way as we are, yet without sin.

> *"Let us therefore draw near with boldness to the throne of grace, that we may receive mercy, and may find grace for help in time of need."* — Hebrews 4:16

## Questions for study

1. What does it mean that a promised rest could still be "open" so long after it was first offered?
2. Where has Scripture recently exposed a thought or intention you'd rather have kept hidden?
3. Does "boldness" toward God come naturally to you, or does it feel presumptuous?
