John the Baptist announced Jesus at the Jordan, saw the heavens open, heard the voice from heaven — and now, sitting in Herod's prison, he sends his own disciples to ask Jesus a startling question: "Are you the one who is coming, or should we look for another?" (11:3). The man who baptized Jesus is not immune to doubt when circumstances get dark enough. Matthew doesn't hide this; he records it plainly.

## Evidence, not scolding

Jesus' answer to John's question is not a rebuke but a list: the blind see, the lame walk, lepers are cleansed, the dead are raised, the poor have good news preached to them (11:4-5). He points John back to what's actually happening rather than demanding blind confidence. Then, once the messengers leave, Jesus turns to the crowd and defends John as more than a prophet — the very messenger predicted to prepare the way. Doubt from prison doesn't erase a life's calling.

> *"Come to me, all you who labor and are heavily burdened, and I will give you rest."* — Matthew 11:28

## Woes for the unimpressed, rest for the tired

The chapter's mood swings sharply. Jesus pronounces judgment on Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum — towns that saw his miracles firsthand and remained unmoved (11:20-24) — and then, without transition, offers the gentlest invitation in the Gospel: come to me, all who are weary, and I will give you rest. His yoke is easy, his burden light (11:30). Unbelief in the face of evidence gets a warning; exhaustion gets an invitation. Matthew places them back to back on purpose.

## Questions for study

1. What does it change about your view of faith to know John the Baptist himself doubted, from prison?
2. Jesus answers doubt with evidence rather than shame. How might that reshape how you respond to your own doubts, or someone else's?
3. Who around you needs to hear "come to me, all who labor and are heavily burdened" this week — and are you willing to be the one who says it?
