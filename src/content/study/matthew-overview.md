Matthew opens with something no other Gospel does: a genealogy, seventeen verses of names, before a single scene has happened. That's not filler — it's the argument. Matthew is writing to readers steeped in Jewish expectation, and he wants the pedigree settled before the story starts: this man descends from Abraham and David, the two names on which every promise about a coming king was built.

## A book built on fulfillment

Matthew quotes the Hebrew Scriptures more than any other Gospel, again and again marking events with some version of "that it might be fulfilled." Birth, exile in Egypt, a voice crying in the wilderness, a king riding a donkey — Matthew keeps stacking evidence that this life wasn't an improvisation. It was the thing Israel's whole story had been pointing at.

## Five sermons, one structure

Underneath Matthew's narrative sits an unusually deliberate architecture: five major blocks of Jesus' teaching, each ending in some form of "when Jesus had finished these sayings" — the Sermon on the Mount (5-7), instructions to the twelve (10), parables of the kingdom (13), teaching on community life (18), and the Olivet Discourse on the end of the age (24-25). Many have noticed the echo of five books of Moses. Whether or not that's deliberate, the effect is the same: Matthew presents Jesus as a teacher whose words carry the weight of law.

## A king who dies and rises

Matthew never lets you forget the royal claim — Magi seeking "he who is born King of the Jews" (2:2) in chapter 2, a mocking sign reading "THE KING OF THE JEWS" over a cross in chapter 27. The crucifixion isn't a detour from that claim; Matthew presents it as the claim's fullest expression. And the book that opened with a promise — *"they shall call his name Immanuel, which is, being interpreted, 'God with us'"* (1:23) — closes with Jesus telling his disciples, *"I am with you always, even to the end of the age"* (28:20).

## Questions for study

1. Why might a genealogy be the right way to open a book aimed at convincing a Jewish audience?
2. What difference does it make to read Jesus' hard sayings as "law" carrying that kind of weight, rather than as suggestions?
3. How does the "King of the Jews" sign over the cross change how you read the crucifixion?
4. Trace "God with us" from 1:23 to 28:20 — what does that bookend claim about the whole story?
